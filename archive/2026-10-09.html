“A swept house left empty doesn't stay empty. Fill it, or something else will.”
October 9th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jesus heals a man who couldn't speak, and some people say he did it with the devil's help. Jesus points out how silly that is: a kingdom fighting itself would collapse. Then he tells a strange little story. An evil spirit is driven out of a person, wanders around, and comes back to find its old home swept and tidy, but empty. So it brings seven worse spirits, and the person ends up worse than before. In Galatians, Paul says Abraham was made right with God by trusting him, and that Christ took the curse of the Law on himself so the blessing of Abraham could reach everyone.

What it means: Cleaning house isn't enough. You can drive out a bad habit, an addiction, a resentment, and still be in danger, because emptiness doesn't stay empty. Something has to move in. Yesterday Jesus promised the Holy Spirit to anyone who asks. Today he shows why we need that gift: a swept heart needs a new tenant. Paul says the same thing from another angle: the Law can point out what's wrong, but only faith fills us with life.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Buddha: passion breaks through an unreflecting mind the way rain breaks through a badly thatched roof.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: water the good seeds in your mind, not the bad ones.
  • Marcus Aurelius: "the soul is dyed by the thoughts."
  • Kahlil Gibran: comfort enters the house as a guest, then becomes its master.
  • Viktor Frankl: an inner emptiness gets filled with whatever is nearby, power, pleasure or conformity.

Carry it today: What have you cleared out of your life, and what have you put in its place?

The theme

  • A divided house falls: Jesus's answer to his critics is common sense: evil doesn't fight itself. The same is true of us. A heart pulled two ways can't stand.
  • The stronger one: A strong man guards his house until someone stronger overpowers him. Jesus is the stronger one, and he comes to free the house, not to rob it.
  • Empty isn't safe: Driving something out is only half the work. A swept, empty house invites worse tenants. Healing means filling the space with something good.
  • Faith fills: Paul says the Law can show us the problem, but it can't give life. Trust in God, like Abraham's, is what lets the Spirit move in.

Yesterday Jesus taught us to ask for the Spirit. Today he tells us what happens to the heart that doesn't: it gets cleaned out and then reoccupied.

1. A House Divided

Jesus has just given a man back his voice. The crowd is amazed, but some critics say he works by Beelzebul, the "prince of demons."

  • Common Sense: "Every kingdom divided against itself will be laid waste and house will fall against house." If Satan were fighting Satan, his kingdom would already be finished.
  • The Finger of God: "If it is by the finger of God that I drive out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you." It's the phrase Pharaoh's magicians used in Exodus when they saw a power greater than their own.

2. The Strong Man and the Stronger One

Jesus describes a strong, armed man guarding his palace, until a stronger one attacks, takes away his armor and divides the spoils.

  • Who's Who: The strong man is evil, holding people captive. The stronger one is Jesus, who frees them. Isaiah had promised that even the captives of a tyrant would be rescued (Isaiah 49:24–25).
  • No Neutral Ground: "Whoever is not with me is against me." In a house with two would-be masters, there's no third option.

3. The Swept, Empty House

This is the heart of today's Gospel. The unclean spirit leaves, wanders through "arid regions," and decides to go back home. It finds the house "swept clean and put in order." So it brings seven spirits worse than itself, "and the last condition of that man is worse than the first."

  • Reform Without Renewal: You can quit something and still be empty. Many people who break a habit find another one rushing in, because the space it left was never filled.
  • Psychology of the Void: Inner emptiness is unstable. Boredom, resentment, distraction and craving all love a vacant room.

4. Abraham's Faith and the Curse

Paul's argument is dense but simple at heart. Abraham "believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6), long before the Law was given. So the true children of Abraham are those who trust God.

  • The Curse of the Law: Paul says anyone who relies on keeping the whole Law lives under a curse, because no one keeps it perfectly.
  • Christ Became a Curse: Quoting Deuteronomy ("cursed be everyone who hangs on a tree"), Paul says Jesus took that curse on himself on the cross, so the blessing of Abraham could reach every nation, and so we could receive the Spirit through faith.

5. Filling the House

Put the readings together and the message is clear: spiritual life isn't mainly about getting rid of things. It's about who lives in us. The Law can sweep the house. Willpower can put it in order. But only the Spirit, received by trust, can live there. The question for today isn't just "What have I given up?" It's "What have I let in?"

For Christians today

The Beelzebul controversy and the parable of the returning spirit have long been read as warnings about half-hearted conversion.

  • The oldest readings: Early teachers read the swept house as the soul cleansed in baptism. Unless it is filled with the Holy Spirit and good works, they warned, it lies open to worse temptations. Jesus's "stronger one" was read as Christ binding Satan by his death and resurrection, the theme of the "harrowing of hell."
  • Catholic: Catholic teaching on conversion stresses that turning from sin must be matched by growth in virtue. The sacrament of Reconciliation forgives, and the life of grace, prayer and the sacraments fills the soul. Today is also the optional memorial of St. John Henry Newman, the English convert and cardinal who wrote "to live is to change, and to be perfect is to have changed often."
  • Protestant and evangelical: Galatians 3:13, "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us," is a central text for the Protestant teaching that Christ died in our place. Evangelical preachers often use the swept house to warn against moral reform without a new heart.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox tradition speaks of guarding the heart with "watchfulness" (nepsis), the practice of noticing thoughts before they settle in. The Jesus Prayer, "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me," is meant to fill the heart so nothing else can take up residence. Today the Orthodox Church honors St. Tikhon, who led the Russian Orthodox mission in North America.
  • Live debates today: Christians argue over how to talk about evil and the demonic: as personal spiritual forces, as social and structural evil, as psychological struggle, or all three. Churches also debate how to support people in recovery, where the risk of the "empty house" is very real.

Carry it today: Name one thing you've let go of this year, and choose one good thing to put in its place.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: the mind as a dwelling.

  • The Hindu lens: The Upanishads speak of the heart as a "city of Brahman" with a small inner space where the divine dwells (Chandogya Upanishad 8.1). The point of spiritual life is to make that inner room a home for God. The Gita says the self can be one's own friend or enemy (6.5–6), the same divided house Jesus warns about.
  • The Jain lens: Jainism teaches that karma is like fine dust that settles on the soul when passions such as anger, pride, deceit and greed leave it open. Stopping the inflow (samvara) and shedding what's already there (nirjara) go together. Cleaning without closing the door doesn't work.

Today is Krishna Chaturdashi, the fourteenth day of the dark half, with the moon in Uttara Phalguni (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). It's Chaturdashi Shraddha in Pitru Paksha, traditionally kept for those who died untimely or violent deaths, and also Masik Shivaratri, the monthly night of Shiva. Tomorrow is Mahalaya Amavasya (Sarva Pitru Amavasya), the new moon that closes Pitru Paksha, and Sharad Navaratri, nine nights honoring the Goddess, begins Sunday, October 11. Friday belongs to the Goddess (Lakshmi and Durga). Chaturdashi (14) is a Jain parva (observance) day, often kept with fasting and extra vows.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit unless noted):

  • Chaturdashi: chatur, "four" (compare Latin quattuor), plus daśa, "ten" (compare Latin decem): the fourteenth lunar day.
  • Shivaratri: Śiva, "auspicious, kind," plus rātri, "night": "Shiva's night."
  • Navaratri: nava, "nine" (compare Latin novem), plus rātri, "night": "nine nights."
  • Mahalaya: mahā, "great" (compare Latin magnus), plus ālaya, "dwelling, abode": "the great abode," on a day about the house.
  • Samvara: sam, "together," plus vṛ, "to cover, close": "closing in," the Jain word for stopping karma from entering (Prakrit and Sanskrit).

Today's readings through a Buddhist lens: guarding the doors of the senses.

  • The six doors: The Buddha taught monks to guard the "doors of the senses," noticing what comes in through the eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body and mind before it takes over. Not to block the world, but to keep the house from being ransacked.
  • Right effort: The Eightfold Path's "right effort" has four parts: prevent unwholesome states from arising, abandon those that have arisen, cultivate wholesome states, and keep them growing. Getting rid of the bad is only half; the other half is growing the good.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A school of philosophy founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, named for the stoa, the painted porch where he taught. Its best-known teachers are Epictetus, a freed slave; Seneca, a Roman senator; and Marcus Aurelius, a Roman emperor. Stoics teach us to focus on what is truly in our power, our judgments, intentions and actions, and to accept the rest calmly. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Such as are thy habitual thoughts, such also will be the character of thy mind; for the soul is dyed by the thoughts." (Meditations 5.16, Long translation.) Marcus's image is cloth soaking up dye. Whatever we let into our minds every day becomes the color of who we are. It's the Stoic version of today's Gospel: don't leave the house empty, because what moves in will shape you.

Today's readings through a Sikh lens: the five thieves and the Name.

  • The five thieves: Sikh teaching names five "thieves" that break into the house of the body: kaam (lust), krodh (anger), lobh (greed), moh (attachment) and ahankar (ego). Gurbani often pictures the body as a house or fortress they try to plunder.
  • Filled with the Name: The remedy isn't just driving them out, but filling the heart with Naam, constant remembrance of God. A heart full of the Name has no room for the thieves.

Hukamnama (the verse drawn each morning from the Guru Granth Sahib at the Golden Temple and read by Sikhs worldwide): Ang 611 (ang, "limb," the reverent word for a page), Raag Sorath, Guru Arjan Dev Ji. After searching everywhere, the Guru concludes that remembering the Lord's Name is the highest truth, the very thing that fills the house.

The time. Jesus's ministry in Galilee took place around AD 28–30 under Roman rule, with Herod Antipas governing Galilee. Luke wrote his Gospel around AD 80–90. Paul wrote Galatians around AD 50–55, about twenty years after Jesus's death.

What scholars broadly agree on. Nearly all historians, including skeptical ones, agree that Jesus was known in his lifetime as an exorcist and healer, and that his opponents explained it by saying he was in league with demons. That charge is the kind of thing early Christians would never have invented. Exorcism was widely practiced in first-century Judaism. The historian Josephus describes a Jewish exorcist named Eleazar driving out a demon in front of the future emperor Vespasian. The Beelzebul debate appears in Mark, Matthew and Luke, so it's among the best-attested stories about Jesus.

What it meant then. The name Beelzebul probably comes from Baal-zebul, "Lord Baal" or "lord of the dwelling," a Canaanite god's title; a mocking Hebrew version, Baal-zebub, "lord of the flies," appears in 2 Kings 1. If "lord of the dwelling" is right, Jesus's images of houses and a strong man guarding his home are a pointed wordplay. For Paul, the argument about Abraham mattered because the Galatians were being told to become Jewish to belong. Abraham, who trusted God before there was any Law, proved that faith was always the way in.

The allegorical readings. Many church fathers read the strong man as Satan and the "stronger one" as Christ, who plundered death itself. Some read the seven worse spirits as the seven deadly sins, and the swept house as a soul that has been baptized but not fed. Others saw the returning spirit as a warning to Israel, or to any community that reforms outwardly without turning inwardly. These are interpretations, not the passage's plain meaning.

What's debated or unknown. Luke says "the finger of God"; Matthew says "the Spirit of God," and scholars debate which was in the original saying. In Galatians 3, two big debates shape how the passage is read: whether Paul's phrase pistis Christou means "faith in Christ" or "the faithfulness of Christ," and what "works of the Law" meant. The "New Perspective on Paul" (E. P. Sanders, James Dunn, N. T. Wright) argues it referred mainly to the markers that set Jews apart, like circumcision and food laws, rather than to good deeds in general.

  • Gregory of Nyssa: He taught that the soul is like a mirror, taking on the likeness of whatever it faces. Turned toward God, it shines with God's beauty; turned away, it reflects darkness. (Paraphrased.) What we face is what fills us.
  • Rumi: In a famous poem he compares being human to a guest house: every morning a new arrival, joy, sorrow, meanness, and he advises welcoming each one as a guide. (Paraphrased.) Jesus adds the warning: be careful who gets to stay.
  • Desert Fathers: A brother asked Abba Moses for a word, and he said, "Go, sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything." (Sayings of the Desert Fathers.) The monks believed you have to stay in the house and tend it, not just clean it and leave.
  • Kahlil Gibran: "Or have you only comfort, and the lust for comfort, that stealthy thing that enters the house a guest, and then becomes a host, and then a master?" (The Prophet, "On Houses.") Gibran's version of the seven worse spirits.
  • Viktor Frankl: He named the "existential vacuum," the inner emptiness of modern life, and warned that people fill it with the will to power, the pursuit of pleasure, or simply doing what others do. (Paraphrased from Man's Search for Meaning.) An empty house never stays empty.
  • Alan Watts: He taught that trying to force your mind to be quiet only stirs it up, the way muddy water clears when you leave it alone. (Paraphrased.) A gentle reminder that filling the house isn't more striving; it's letting the right guest in.
