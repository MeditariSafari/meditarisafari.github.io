November 8th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: At the end of his life, Joshua gathers all the tribes at Shechem. He retells the whole story, from Abraham to the promised land, and then puts a choice to them: "Choose this day whom you will serve." He makes his own choice plain: "As for me and my household, we will serve the Lord." The people answer that they will too. In Matthew, Jesus tells of ten bridesmaids waiting with lamps for a wedding procession. The bridegroom is late; they all doze off. At midnight the shout goes up. Five have brought extra oil and light their lamps; five have not, and while they run to buy more, the door is shut. "Keep awake, therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour."

What it means: Joshua asks for a choice; Jesus asks what it takes to keep that choice through a long night. Saying yes once is easy. Keeping the lamp burning when the wait is long, when you're tired, when nothing seems to happen, takes oil you have to gather ahead of time: prayer, practice, love. And no one else can carry your oil for you. On Diwali, as millions light lamps against the dark, the parable asks: is your lamp filled?

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Remember how long thou hast been putting off these things, and how often thou hast received an opportunity from the gods, and yet dost not use it." (Meditations 2.4, Long.)
  • Buddha: He taught: be a lamp unto yourselves (Digha Nikaya 16, paraphrased).
  • Hindu: On Diwali, lamps are lit to welcome Lakshmi and to celebrate light's victory over darkness.
  • Judaism: Hillel asked: if not now, when? (Pirkei Avot 1:14, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Is your lamp filled for the long wait?

The theme

  • Choose this day: Joshua puts the question to the whole people.
  • As for me and my household: Joshua's own answer.
  • The bridegroom delayed: The wait is longer than anyone expected.
  • Extra oil: The wise come prepared for the long night.

For weeks the lectionary has followed Israel from the wilderness into the land. Today Joshua's story ends with a choice, and Jesus' parable asks how we keep that choice alive.

1. Gathered at Shechem

Joshua gathers the tribes at Shechem and retells their story, beginning with Abraham "beyond the River."

  • Shechem: Where Abraham first built an altar in the land (Genesis 12:6–7), and where Joseph's bones would be buried (Joshua 24:32).
  • Remembering first: Before asking for a choice, Joshua tells them everything God has done.

2. Choose This Day

"Now if you are unwilling to serve the Lord, choose this day whom you will serve," "but as for me and my household, we will serve the Lord."

  • A real choice: Joshua doesn't force them. Faith must be chosen.
  • Leading by example: Joshua speaks for himself first.

3. We Will Serve the Lord

"No, we will serve the Lord!" "The Lord our God we will serve, and him we will obey."

  • A covenant: Joshua writes it down and sets up a great stone as a witness.
  • Joshua's warning: He tells them it won't be easy (24:19). Choosing is the beginning, not the end.

4. The Bridegroom Is Delayed

"As the bridegroom was delayed, all of them became drowsy and slept. But at midnight there was a shout."

  • Everyone sleeps: The wise aren't the ones who stayed awake; they're the ones who came prepared.
  • The long wait: Matthew's community was waiting longer than expected for Christ's return.

5. Oil for the Lamp

"The foolish said to the wise, 'Give us some of your oil, for our lamps are going out.'" The wise say no, which can sound harsh. But some things can't be borrowed: a life of prayer, a habit of love, a heart kept faithful over time. "Those who were ready went with him into the wedding banquet, and the door was shut." Joshua asks Israel to choose; Jesus shows what it takes to keep choosing through a long night. The oil is gathered day by day, before the shout comes. Paul adds a word of comfort for those grieving: we do not "grieve as others do who have no hope." "Encourage one another with these words."

For Christians today

"Choose this day" and the ten bridesmaids are two of the most quoted passages in Christian life.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine read the oil as love, which cannot be borrowed from another. Some fathers read the lamps as faith and the oil as good works.
  • "As for me and my house": Joshua 24:15 hangs on the walls of countless Christian homes.
  • Catholic: Today is the 32nd Sunday, as the Church year nears its end and the readings turn toward the last things.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Revival preachers used Joshua 24:15 to call for personal decision, and the bridesmaids to urge readiness.
  • Advent hymns: "Wake, awake, for night is flying" (Philipp Nicolai, 1599) is based on this parable.
  • Live debates today: How to read the "shut door" alongside God's mercy is debated.

Carry it today: Add one drop of oil today: one prayer, one act of love.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Remember how long thou hast been putting off these things, and how often thou hast received an opportunity from the gods, and yet dost not use it. Thou must now at last perceive of what universe thou art a part, and of what administrator of the universe thy existence is an efflux, and that a limit of time is fixed for thee, which if thou dost not use for clearing away the clouds from thy mind, it will go and thou wilt go, and it will never return." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.4, Long translation.) Marcus scolds himself for putting off the work of the soul. Time is limited, and it doesn't come back. Joshua says "choose this day"; the bridesmaids learn that the door does shut. The time to gather oil is now.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Diwali.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Diwali, the festival of lights. Families light rows of clay lamps (diyas), worship Lakshmi, goddess of prosperity, and celebrate the victory of light over darkness and good over evil, remembering Rama's return home. In Gujarat, Sharda Pujan honors the goddess of learning, and businesses bless new account books.
  • The Jain lens: For Jains, Diwali marks the night Mahavira attained final liberation (nirvana). Since the light of knowledge had gone out, people lit lamps in its place.

Today is Krishna Chaturdashi (ending at 11:27 AM India time), then Amavasya, the new moon, with the moon moving from Chitra into Svati (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Diwali: from deepavali, "row of lamps."
  • Diya: "lamp."
  • Amavasya: "the new moon," the darkest night.
  • Svati: "the independent one," the star Arcturus.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; Matthew wrote around AD 80–90; 1 Thessalonians around AD 50.

What scholars broadly agree on. Joshua 24 records a covenant ceremony at Shechem, possibly reflecting an ancient tradition of covenant renewal. Wedding customs in Jesus' time included a nighttime procession with torches or oil lamps as the groom came to bring the bride home.

What it meant then. For Israel, the Shechem covenant bound the tribes to one God. For Matthew's community, the parable spoke to a church waiting longer than it expected for Christ's return.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the ten bridesmaids as the whole church, the oil as love or good works, and the bridegroom as Christ.

What's debated or unknown. Whether "lamps" were small oil lamps or torches is debated.
