“Joshua builds an altar on Mount Ebal and reads every word of the law aloud to the whole people: women, children and foreigners included.”
November 6th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: After the first victories in the land, Joshua stops everything. He builds an altar of uncut stones on Mount Ebal, near Shechem, and writes a copy of Moses' law on stones. Then, with the tribes standing on two mountainsides and the ark in the valley between, he reads every word aloud. The text is careful to say who heard it: the whole assembly, "and the women, and the little ones, and the aliens who resided among them." The psalm calls each generation to teach the next, even "the children yet unborn."

What it means: God's word was not kept for a priestly few. Joshua read all of it, blessings and curses alike, to all the people: men and women, adults and children, citizens and foreigners. Nobody was too small or too much of an outsider to hear it. That's a challenge for any community of faith: who gets to hear, and who gets left out?

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Men exist for the sake of one another. Teach them then or bear with them." (Meditations 8.59, Long.)
  • Confucius: He said that in teaching there should be no distinction of classes (Analects 15.39, paraphrased).
  • Sikh: The Guru Granth Sahib includes hymns by Hindu and Muslim saints alongside the Gurus, open to all who come.
  • Islam: The Qur'an says God made people into nations and tribes so they might know one another (49:13, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Is anyone left out of your "everyone"?

The theme

  • An altar on Ebal: Worship comes before settling the land.
  • Written on stones: The law is made public and permanent.
  • Every word: Blessings and curses, nothing held back.
  • Everyone: Women, children and foreigners hear it too.

This week Israel crossed the Jordan, took Jericho, and ate the grain of the land. Today Joshua pauses to renew the covenant before the whole people.

1. An Altar on Mount Ebal

"Then Joshua built on Mount Ebal an altar to the Lord, the God of Israel."

  • Uncut stones: Moses had commanded an altar of whole stones, untouched by iron tools (Deuteronomy 27:5–6). Worship is shaped by God, not human craft.
  • At the center of the land: Ebal and Gerizim stand above Shechem, where Abraham first built an altar in Canaan (Genesis 12:6–7).

2. Written on the Stones

"And there, in the presence of the Israelites, Joshua wrote on the stones a copy of the law of Moses that he had written."

  • Public: The law is set up where everyone can see it.
  • Lasting: Stone outlasts memory. The covenant is meant to endure.

3. Blessings and Curses

"He read all the words of the law, blessings and curses, according to all that is written in the book of the law."

  • Two mountains: Half the tribes faced Gerizim, the mountain of blessing, and half Ebal, the mountain of curse (Deuteronomy 11:29).
  • Nothing edited out: Joshua reads the hard parts too.

4. Not a Word Left Out

"There was not a word of all that Moses commanded that Joshua did not read before all the assembly of Israel."

  • Complete: Faithfulness means telling the whole story, not just the comfortable parts.
  • Before all: The reading is for the whole community, not a select few.

5. The Women, the Little Ones, and the Aliens

"And the women, and the little ones, and the aliens who resided among them." The list is deliberate. In the ancient world, public readings of law were usually for adult men. Here, women, children and resident foreigners stand in the assembly and hear every word. The psalm adds a further circle: "the children yet unborn." The covenant is wide. In Jesus' day, his table had the same shape, open to tax collectors, women, children and outsiders. The question it leaves us with is simple and searching: when we say "everyone," who do we actually mean?

For Christians today

The reading at Ebal has shaped how Christians think about Scripture in public life.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers saw the altar of uncut stones as Christ, the stone not cut by human hands (Daniel 2:34), and the reading of the law as the gospel proclaimed to all nations.
  • Reformation: The Reformers pressed for the Bible to be read aloud in the language of the people, so that everyone, not just clergy, could hear it.
  • Catholic: Since Vatican II, Catholic Masses read much more of the Bible aloud in the local language on a three-year cycle.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many churches practice public Scripture reading, and some hold "Bible marathons" reading the whole Bible aloud.
  • Live debates today: Who gets to read and preach in church, and how fully difficult texts are read aloud, are live questions across traditions.

Carry it today: Include someone today who is usually left out.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Men exist for the sake of one another. Teach them then or bear with them." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.59, Long translation.) Two short sentences from an emperor. People exist for each other: so teach them, or put up with them, but don't shut them out. Joshua chose to teach everyone, every word.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC. Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. Joshua 8:30–35 fulfills Deuteronomy 27 and was likely placed here by the editors who shaped Joshua and Deuteronomy together. Shechem, between Ebal and Gerizim, was an important early Israelite site.

What it meant then. For Israel, the ceremony said the land was held under God's covenant, and that everyone, including outsiders, belonged to that covenant.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the uncut stones as Christ and as believers built into God's house as living stones (1 Peter 2:5).

What's debated or unknown. A structure on Mount Ebal excavated in the 1980s has been identified by some as Joshua's altar; others see it as a farmhouse or watchtower. The question is still debated.
