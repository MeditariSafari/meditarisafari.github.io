“Israel keeps its first Passover in the promised land, and the manna that fed them for forty years stops. Now they eat from the land itself.”
November 5th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: For forty years in the wilderness, God fed Israel with manna, bread from heaven that appeared every morning. Now they've crossed the Jordan. At Gilgal they keep the Passover, the meal that remembers their escape from Egypt. The next day they eat grain from the fields of their new land. And on that very day, the manna stops. It never comes again. The psalm calls each generation to tell the next the story of what God has done, "so that they should set their hope in God."

What it means: The manna was a gift for a season, and the season ended. God's provision didn't stop; it changed form. What had come from the sky now comes from the soil, through planting and harvest. Many of us have known a "manna" season, when help came every day without our doing. Growing up in faith can mean that the miracles become ordinary, and God feeds us through work, land and community.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Think not so much of what thou hast not as of what thou hast: but of the things which thou hast select the best and then reflect how eagerly they would have been sought, if thou hadst them not." (Meditations 7.27, Long.)
  • Tao Te Ching: It says one who knows they have enough is rich (Ch. 33, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: He taught that contentment is the greatest wealth (Dhammapada 204, paraphrased).
  • Judaism: Ben Zoma taught: who is rich? One who is happy with their portion (Pirkei Avot 4:1, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Where is God feeding you now in a new way, after the old provision ended?

This week Israel crossed the Jordan, set up memorial stones, and faced Jericho. Today the lectionary steps back to the quiet moment between: Passover, and the end of the manna.

1. Passover at Gilgal

"While the Israelites were camped in Gilgal, they kept the Passover in the evening on the fourteenth day of the month in the plains of Jericho."

  • Remembering first: Before any battle, Israel stops to remember how God freed them.
  • Full circle: The first Passover was eaten in Egypt in haste; this one is eaten in the land of promise.

2. The Produce of the Land

"On the day after the Passover, on that very day, they ate the produce of the land, unleavened cakes and roasted grain."

  • First fruits: The first food from the new land is a sign that the promise has come true.
  • Unleavened bread: The same bread as the night they left Egypt, now made from their own grain.

3. The Manna Ceased

"The manna ceased on the day they ate the produce of the land, and the Israelites no longer had manna."

  • A season's gift: Manna was never meant to last forever. It carried them until the land could.
  • Same God, new form: The God who fed them from the sky now feeds them through the soil.

4. Tell the Coming Generation

"We will not hide them from their children; we will tell to the coming generation the glorious deeds of the Lord."

  • Memory as faith: Most of Israel's children never saw manna. They learn of it through the story.
  • Hope: The goal is "that they should set their hope in God, and not forget the works of God."

5. When the Old Provision Ends

The manna stops the day the harvest begins. There is no gap, but there is a change. Israel moves from receiving to farming, from wilderness to settled life, from daily miracle to ordinary work. Many of us know a version of this: a mentor moves away, a job ends, a season of easy faith passes. Endings can feel like abandonment. Joshua 5 tells us otherwise. God's care doesn't stop when its form changes. The same Lord who sent manna now gives grain, and the task is to recognize God's hand in ordinary bread. Jesus would later call himself "the bread of life," linking the manna to himself.

For Christians today

The manna and Passover are central images in Christian worship.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers saw the end of the manna as a figure of the Eucharist: the manna of the wilderness gives way to the true bread from heaven (John 6). Origen taught that when we enter the "land" of spiritual maturity, we are fed by the Word itself.
  • Catholic: Catholics read the manna as a foreshadowing of the Eucharist, which the church calls "food for the journey."
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many preachers use the end of the manna to speak of seasons of change, when God calls believers from dependence to responsibility.
  • The Lord's Prayer: "Give us this day our daily bread" echoes the manna, given one day at a time.
  • Live debates today: How to understand the manna (a miracle, a natural substance like tamarisk resin, or both) is still discussed.

Carry it today: Thank God for one ordinary provision you take for granted.

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Think not so much of what thou hast not as of what thou hast: but of the things which thou hast select the best and then reflect how eagerly they would have been sought, if thou hadst them not. At the same time however take care that thou dost not through being so pleased with them accustom thyself to overvalue them, so as to be disturbed if ever thou shouldst not have them." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 7.27, Long translation.) Marcus asks us to be grateful for what we have without clinging to it. Israel had to let the manna go. Gratitude for each gift, and freedom when it ends, is the Stoic way through change.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; Psalm 78 is likely from the monarchy; Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. Gilgal was an early Israelite worship site near Jericho. Joshua 5 ties the entry into the land to Passover, making the conquest a liturgical event. Some scholars connect manna with a sweet resin produced in the Sinai by insects on tamarisk trees.

What it meant then. For later Israel, the story tied their worship and their bread to God's faithfulness at the beginning.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the manna as Christ, the true bread from heaven, and the end of the manna as the passage from the old covenant to the new.

What's debated or unknown. The exact location of Gilgal is not known for certain.
