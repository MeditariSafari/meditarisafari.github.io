“Israel marches around Jericho in silence for seven days, then shouts, and the walls fall. The church at Antioch fasts and prays, then sends out Paul and Barnabas.”
November 3rd, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jericho's gates are locked tight. God tells Joshua the city is already given, but the way to take it is strange: march around it once a day for six days, priests blowing ram's horns, the ark in the middle, and the people saying nothing at all. On the seventh day, march around seven times. Then Joshua shouts, "Shout! For the Lord has given you the city," the people raise a great cry, and the wall falls flat. In Acts, the church at Antioch is worshiping and fasting when the Spirit speaks, and they send Paul and Barnabas out into the world.

What it means: The walls didn't fall on day one. They fell after six days of silent, faithful walking. Most of faith is that: showing up, walking the circuit, keeping quiet, trusting what God has already promised. The shout comes at the end. The church at Antioch, too, heard the Spirit only after worship and fasting.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Nothing great, said Epictetus, is produced suddenly, since not even the grape or the fig is." (Discourses 1.15, Long.)
  • Tao Te Ching: A tree as wide as a man's embrace grows from a tiny shoot; a journey of a thousand miles begins beneath one's feet (Ch. 64, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: He taught that drop by drop the water pot is filled, and so the wise fill themselves with good, little by little (Dhammapada 122, paraphrased).
  • Judaism: Rabbi Tarfon taught that you are not required to finish the work, but neither are you free to stop doing it (Pirkei Avot 2:16, paraphrased).

Carry it today: What wall are you circling in faithful silence, still waiting for the day to shout?

The theme

  • Shut up tight: Jericho seems impossible to enter.
  • Already given: God says the city is handed over before a single step.
  • Silence: Six days of marching without a word.
  • The shout: On the seventh day, the walls fall.

Over the past few days Israel crossed the Jordan and set up memorial stones. Today they face their first city, and learn that the land is taken God's way.

1. Shut Up Inside and Out

"Now Jericho was shut up inside and out because of the Israelites; no one came out, and no one went in."

  • Fear on both sides: Jericho is locked down in fear. The walls are as much about terror as strength.
  • No way in: By any human measure, the first obstacle is impossible.

2. I Have Handed It Over

"See, I have handed Jericho over to you, along with its king and soldiers."

  • Past tense: God speaks as if it's already done. The marching is not to earn the city but to receive it.
  • Promise first: Faith lives between the promise and the fulfillment.

3. Not a Word

"You shall not shout or let your voice be heard, nor shall you utter a word until the day I tell you to shout."

  • Holy silence: Six days of walking without complaint, boast or argument.
  • No grumbling: Their parents complained in the wilderness. This generation learns to walk quietly and trust.

4. Seven Times on the Seventh Day

"On the seventh day they rose early, at dawn, and marched around the city in the same manner seven times."

  • Seven: The number of completion and of creation. The conquest is framed like a new week of creation.
  • "In the same manner": Faithfulness is often repetition. Same walk, same path, until the day it changes.

5. Shout!

"Shout! For the Lord has given you the city." "So the people shouted, and the trumpets were blown," "and the wall fell down flat." The shout doesn't knock the walls down; God does. But the people's voice joins God's action at the right moment. In Acts, the same pattern: the church worships and fasts, and then the Spirit speaks, "Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul." After fasting and praying, "they laid their hands on them and sent them off." Silence and patience come first; the shout and the sending come when God says. The question for us is whether we can keep walking the circuit faithfully, in silence, until then.

For Christians today

Jericho is one of the best-known stories in the Bible, and one of the hardest.

  • The oldest readings: Origen read the trumpets as the preaching of the gospel, and the falling walls as the collapse of everything that resists God in the human heart. Hebrews 11:30 says the walls fell by faith, after seven days of circling.
  • Spirituals: "Joshua Fit the Battle of Jericho" became a song of hope among enslaved African Americans: walls that seemed permanent could fall.
  • Hard questions: The rest of Joshua 6 describes the city's destruction, which troubles many readers. Scholars across traditions note that the conquest stories are shaped by later theology and should not be read as a model for violence. Jesus' way, in Matthew's readings this week, is the way of the servant.
  • Catholic: Today's Gospel is the great banquet, where those first invited refuse and the poor are brought in. St. Martin de Porres lived it.
  • Live debates today: How to read the conquest stories honestly is a live discussion among Jews and Christians alike.

Carry it today: Keep walking one faithful circuit today, without complaint.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Nothing great, said Epictetus, is produced suddenly, since not even the grape or the fig is. If you say to me now that you want a fig, I will answer to you that it requires time: let it flower first, then put forth fruit, and then ripen." (Epictetus, Discourses 1.15, Long translation.) Epictetus tells an impatient student that inner change takes seasons, like fruit. Israel marched six days with nothing to show for it. The fig flowers before it fruits, and the walls stand until the seventh day.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC, but was likely shaped much later, in the 7th or 6th century BC. Acts was written around AD 80–90.

What scholars broadly agree on. Archaeology at Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) suggests the city was largely unoccupied or very small in the late 13th century BC; its famous walls are much older. Most scholars read Joshua 6 as a liturgical story, with priests, the ark, trumpets and seven days, more than a battle report.

What it meant then. For Israel in exile, the story said the land was God's gift, not their own conquest. For Luke, Antioch's sending marked the gospel going out to the nations.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the seven days and trumpets as the preaching that brings down pride.

What's debated or unknown. How much history lies behind the conquest is debated. Many scholars think Israel emerged more gradually within Canaan.
