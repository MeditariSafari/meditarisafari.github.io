“You can't pray "our Father" and then decide who gets a seat at the table.”
The five-minute version

The readings: The disciples watch Jesus pray and ask him to teach them. He gives them a short prayer: Father, may your name be holy, may your kingdom come, give us our daily bread, forgive us as we forgive, and don't bring us to the test. In Galatians, Paul tells a hard story. In Antioch, Jewish and non-Jewish believers ate together, and Peter ate with everyone, until some important men arrived from Jerusalem. Then Peter quietly moved to the other table, afraid of what they'd think. Paul called him out in front of everyone.

What it means: The prayer begins with "Father," and in Matthew's version "Our Father." If God is our Father, everyone at the table is family. Peter knew that. He'd even had a vision about it. But when the wrong people were watching, fear won. It's the same approval trap Paul warned about on Monday, now caught in the act. Praying the Lord's Prayer honestly means letting it rearrange who we eat with, who we forgive and whose opinion we fear.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Bhagavad Gita: the wise see the priest, the cow, the elephant, the dog and the outcast as "all one."
  • Sikhism: in the langar, everyone sits on the floor and eats the same food, whatever their caste or faith.
  • Islam: in prayer, worshipers stand shoulder to shoulder in straight rows, rich and poor side by side.
  • Confucius: "To see what is right and not to do it is want of courage."
  • Martin Luther King Jr.: he called Sunday morning one of the most segregated hours in America.

Carry it today: Whose opinion makes you move to a different table?

The theme

  • The prayer starts with family: Jesus teaches us to call God "Father." That one word makes every other person a brother or sister, including the ones we'd rather not sit with.
  • Fear changes seats: Peter didn't change his beliefs in Antioch. He changed his seat, because he was afraid of what important people would think. Most of our failures of love look like that: not a new opinion, just a quiet step away.
  • Daily bread is shared bread: The prayer asks for "our" daily bread, not mine. Where we eat, and who we eat with, is where the prayer gets tested.

On Monday Paul warned against trying to please people. On Tuesday Mary chose to sit at Jesus's feet. Today we learn how to pray, and then watch one of the greatest apostles fail at the very thing the prayer asks.

1. "Lord, Teach Us to Pray"

The disciples don't ask Jesus to teach them miracles or preaching. They watch him pray and want what he has. Luke shows Jesus praying before nearly every turning point: at his baptism, before choosing the twelve, at the Transfiguration, in Gethsemane.

  • A Short Prayer: Luke's version is only five requests long, shorter than the version in Matthew that most churches use. It isn't a magic formula. It's a shape for the heart.
  • "Father": Jesus probably used the Aramaic word Abba, the word a child used for their dad. Paul still quotes it in Greek letters to non-Jewish churches (Galatians 4:6, Romans 8:15). Prayer starts with belonging, not performance.

2. Five Requests, Five Surrenders

Each line hands something back to God.

  • "Hallowed be your name... your kingdom come": My name, my kingdom, my reputation come second.
  • "Give us each day our daily bread": Enough for today, not a warehouse for the future, like the manna in the desert that spoiled if anyone hoarded it.
  • "Forgive us our sins, for we ourselves forgive everyone in debt to us": The only line with a condition. We can't receive what we refuse to give.
  • "Do not subject us to the final test": An honest admission that we're weaker than we think. Peter is about to prove it.

3. Peter at the Wrong Table

In Antioch, the church did something radical: Jewish and non-Jewish believers ate together, ignoring the old food rules that kept them apart. Peter joined in. Then "some people came from James," and Peter "drew back and kept himself separate, because he was afraid."

  • He Knew Better: Acts 10 tells of Peter's vision, God telling him not to call anyone unclean. He had already eaten in a non-Jew's house. This wasn't ignorance. It was fear.
  • It Spread: "The rest of the Jews also acted hypocritically along with him, with the result that even Barnabas was carried away." One respected person changing seats can split a whole church.

4. Paul at His Most Uncomfortable

Paul confronted Peter "to his face" and "in front of all." It's an awkward scene: two founders of the church, in public conflict.

  • Not About Food: For Paul this was the Gospel itself. If non-Jewish believers had to become Jewish to share the table, then grace wasn't enough. A divided table preached a different gospel.
  • Love Can Confront: Paul's challenge was hard, but it was an act of love for the church and for Peter. Sometimes keeping the peace is just keeping the fear.

5. Praying What We Live

Psalm 117 calls "all you nations" to praise God. Today's memorial of the Rosary invites slow, repeated prayer. Put it all together and the point is simple: the Lord's Prayer isn't only words we say. It's a table we sit at. Every time we pray "our," we're asked who we've quietly left out, and whose disapproval we're afraid of. Peter later found his courage again. The prayer is how we find ours.

For Christians today

The Lord's Prayer is the one prayer nearly every Christian on earth shares, and Antioch is one of the earliest church fights on record.

  • The oldest readings: Early teachers like Tertullian, Cyprian and Origen wrote whole treatises on the Lord's Prayer. Cyprian stressed that Jesus taught us to say "our" Father and "give us," not "my," because Christians pray as one body. The Didache, a church manual from around AD 100, already tells believers to pray it three times a day.
  • Catholic: The Catechism calls the Lord's Prayer "the summary of the whole gospel" (quoting Tertullian), and it is prayed at every Mass right before Communion, at the table. Today's memorial of the Rosary, where the Our Father begins each decade, was set by Pope Pius V after the Battle of Lepanto in 1571.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther taught the Lord's Prayer in his Small Catechism line by line, and Protestants added the ending "for thine is the kingdom, the power and the glory," which comes from early church use but isn't in the oldest copies of Matthew or Luke. Paul's stand against Peter became a favorite text for the Reformers: grace, not rules, decides who belongs.
  • Orthodox: The Lord's Prayer is prayed aloud by the whole congregation at every Divine Liturgy, just before Communion. The Orthodox honor Peter and Paul together on one feast day, June 29, as if to say the Antioch argument didn't break the family.
  • Live debates today: Antioch still stings. Churches today argue over who's welcome at the Communion table, how to share it across denominations, and why Sunday worship in America is still so divided by race. Many Christians also argue over "forgive us our debts," and whether it calls for practical forgiveness of financial debts for the poor.

Carry it today: The next time you pray "our Father," think of one person you'd rather not sit next to, and pray it for them too.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy started in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, who taught under a painted porch (stoa). Its most famous voices are Epictetus, a freed slave; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and Marcus Aurelius, an emperor. It teaches us to separate what's up to us (our judgments, choices and actions) from what isn't (other people's opinions, events, reputation), and to put our energy into the first. Its four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control, and it held that all people belong to one human community.

Epictetus put the approval trap bluntly: if you want to make progress, be content to seem foolish and ignorant to others about outside things, and don't wish to seem to know anything. (Enchiridion 13, paraphrased.) Peter in Antioch wanted to look right to the visitors from Jerusalem. Epictetus would tell him the price of wisdom is being willing to look wrong to the important people.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: purity and oneness.

  • The Hindu lens: Like Peter's food laws, Hindu tradition long kept rules of purity about who could eat with whom. Reformers such as the 12th-century saint Basava, and later Gandhi, challenged caste exclusion, and Gandhi called the so-called untouchables Harijan, "people of God." The Gita's vision of seeing all beings as one (Gita 5.18) is the deepest answer.
  • The Jain lens: Jainism teaches anekantavada, "many-sidedness," that no single viewpoint holds the whole truth, and it was one of the early traditions in India to accept followers from every caste. Paul and Peter each saw part of the truth; it took the argument to see it whole.

Today is Krishna Dwadashi, the twelfth day of the dark half, with the moon in Magha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). It's Dwadashi Shraddha in Pitru Paksha. Still Pitru Paksha, the fortnight of honoring the dead, which ends with Mahalaya Amavasya (the new moon). Wednesday belongs to Ganesha, the remover of obstacles.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Harijan: Hari, a name of Vishnu (God), plus jana, "people": "people of God."
  • Anekantavada: an-, "not," plus eka, "one," plus anta, "end, side," plus vada, "teaching": "the teaching of not-one-sidedness."
  • Dwadashi: dvā, "two" (compare Latin duo), plus daśa, "ten": the twelfth lunar day.
  • Magha: "the mighty" or "the bountiful," a nakshatra linked to the ancestors (pitṛs), fitting in Pitru Paksha.
  • Pitru Paksha: pitṛ, "father" or "ancestor" (same root as Latin pater and English father), plus paksha, "half-month." A good word on a day about "our Father."

The time. The Antioch incident probably happened around AD 48–49, about fifteen years after Jesus's death. Paul's trip to Jerusalem "after fourteen years" (Galatians 2:1) is usually linked to the meeting Acts 15 calls the Council of Jerusalem, around AD 49, though some scholars match it to an earlier visit. Antioch in Syria was one of the largest cities of the Roman Empire, and it was where Jesus's followers were first called "Christians" (Acts 11:26). Paul wrote Galatians a few years later, around AD 50–55. Luke wrote his Gospel around AD 80–90.

What scholars broadly agree on. Almost all historians accept that the Antioch confrontation really happened. It's an embarrassing story for the early church, and nobody would have invented a public fight between Peter and Paul. They agree the dispute was over table fellowship: Jewish food laws made sharing meals with non-Jews a real question, and "men from James" (James, the brother of Jesus, who led the Jerusalem church) represented a stricter view. On the Lord's Prayer, most scholars think Luke's shorter version is closer to the original length and Matthew's wording closer to how churches prayed it. They also agree that "Abba" behind "Father" was distinctive of Jesus's own prayer. Psalm 117 is the shortest chapter in the Bible.

What it meant then. For Luke, the prayer marks Jesus's followers as a community with its own way of praying, the way John the Baptist's disciples had theirs (Luke 11:1). For Paul, Antioch was a test of whether non-Jewish believers were full members of the family or second-class guests. He told the story to show the Galatians that even Peter had to bow to "the truth of the gospel."

The allegorical readings. Church fathers read the Lord's Prayer as a map of the whole spiritual life. Augustine matched its seven petitions (in Matthew's version) to the seven Beatitudes and the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit. The Antioch story embarrassed some early readers so much that Jerome, following Origen, suggested Peter and Paul had staged the argument as a teaching moment. Augustine wrote to Jerome rejecting that: Scripture doesn't lie, he argued, and Peter really did need correcting. Augustine's view won.

What's debated or unknown. Paul never tells us how the Antioch argument ended. Many scholars suspect Paul lost that round, since soon afterward he set out on his travels without Barnabas (Acts 15:39). The doxology "for thine is the kingdom" isn't in the oldest manuscripts and was likely added from church worship. The Rosary is traditionally credited to St. Dominic, but historians see it developing gradually over the Middle Ages.

  • Joseph Campbell: One stage of the hero's journey he called "atonement with the father," which he liked to read as "at-one-ment": the moment the hero stops fearing the father figure and finds himself one with him. (Our summary of his ideas, not his words.) Peter's change of tables wasn't about food. His group identity felt threatened, and the ego moved him.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: He taught "interbeing," that nothing exists by itself, and that we "inter-are" with every other person, the way a flower contains the sun, the rain and the soil. (Paraphrased.) An "our Father" prayer is an interbeing prayer: there is no "my" bread apart from yours.
  • Gurdjieff: He taught that most people are "machines," driven by habit and outside pressure, not by real choice, and that waking up means catching ourselves in the act. (Paraphrased.) Peter in Antioch is the machine at work: the important men walk in, and he moves before he thinks.
  • Tao Te Ching: "To those who are good (to me), I am good; and to those who are not good (to me), I am also good;--and thus (all) get to be good." (Ch. 49, Legge translation.) The sage sets one table for everyone.
  • Martin Luther King Jr.: He often said that eleven o'clock on Sunday morning is "the most segregated hour" in Christian America, including on Meet the Press in 1960. (Short phrase; wording varied across his talks.) Antioch's divided table, nineteen centuries later.
  • Confucius: "To see what is right and not to do it is want of courage." (Analects 2.24, Legge translation.) Peter saw what was right. In Antioch he lacked the courage.
  • Nelson Mandela: On Robben Island he learned Afrikaans and studied the history of his jailers, and he insisted on treating the warders with dignity, believing even an oppressor could be won over.   • Marcus Aurelius: "How much trouble he avoids who does not look to see what his neighbour says or does or thinks, but only to what he does himself, that it may be just and pure." (Meditations 4.18, Long translation.) Peter's trouble began when he looked over his shoulder at the men from James.
  • Ram Dass: He taught the practice of seeing every person as a soul first, beyond their role, race or religion, and treating each encounter as a meeting with the divine. (Paraphrased.) It's the practice that would have kept Peter in his seat.
  • Buddha: "The fault of others is easily perceived, but that of oneself is difficult to perceive; a man winnows his neighbour's faults like chaff, but his own fault he hides."   • Bhagavad Gita: "To him who wisely sees, / The Brahman with his scrolls and sanctities, / The cow, the elephant, the unclean dog, / The Outcast gorging dog's meat, are all one." (Ch. 5, Arnold translation, The Song Celestial.) The wise eat at one table.

  • Rumi: The Sufi poet wrote that the lamps are different but the light is the same, and that lovers of God come from every faith and every nation. (Paraphrased.) Antioch's two tables were lit by the same light.
  • Desert Fathers: When the brothers gathered to judge one who had sinned, Abba Moses came carrying a leaking jug (in some tellings, a basket of sand) and said his own sins were running out behind him unseen, and yet he'd come to judge another. The brothers forgave the man. (Sayings of the Desert Fathers, paraphrased.) "Forgive us, as we forgive."
  • Kahlil Gibran: "You pray in your distress and in your need; would that you might pray also in the fullness of your joy and in your days of abundance. For what is prayer but the expansion of yourself into the living ether?" (The Prophet, "On Prayer.") Prayer as expansion: the self grows until the table has room for everyone.
  • Viktor Frankl: From the camps he concluded that there are only two "races" of people, the decent and the indecent, and that both are found in every group, guards and prisoners alike.   • Alan Watts: He liked to say that the menu is not the meal: words and rules about reality aren't reality itself. (Paraphrased.) Peter kept the menu, the food rules, and walked away from the meal, the family at the table.
