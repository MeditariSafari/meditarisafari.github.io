“On the last Sunday of the church year, the King turns out to be a shepherd, and he is found in the hungry, the stranger, the sick and the prisoner.”
November 22nd, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: The church year ends with a king, but a strange one. In Ezekiel, God fires the bad shepherds and says, "I myself will search for my sheep." He'll find the lost, bring back the strays, bandage the injured. In Matthew, Jesus tells his last parable before the cross. The Son of Man sits on his throne and gathers all the nations. To those on his right he says: "I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me something to drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me." They're baffled: "Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry and gave you food?" And the King answers: "Just as you did it to one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did it to me."

What it means: Neither group recognized him. The righteous didn't know they were serving Christ; they just helped people. The King of the universe hides in the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner. That's where he's found.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye." (Meditations 6.30, Long.) The emperor warned himself against the throne.
  • Judaism: Visiting the sick, feeding the hungry and welcoming guests are mitzvot, walking in God's ways (Sotah 14a, paraphrased).
  • Islam: A hadith says God will ask on the Day of Judgment, "I was sick and you did not visit me" (Muslim, paraphrased).
  • Sikhism: The langar feeds anyone who comes, as if serving the Guru.

Yesterday, Jesus pointed to his disciples as his family. Today, he says the least of these are his brothers and sisters too.

1. I Myself Will Search

"For thus says the Lord God: I myself will search for my sheep and will sort them out."

  • No more middlemen: The kings failed; God will do it himself.
  • The lost and the strays: "I will seek the lost, and I will bring back the strays, and I will bind up the injured, and I will strengthen the weak."

2. The King on His Throne

The Son of Man comes in glory with all the angels, sits on his throne, and gathers all the nations before him, separating them as a shepherd separates sheep from goats (Matthew 25:31–33, paraphrased).

  • King and shepherd: The two images of Ezekiel 34 meet in one person.
  • All the nations: Not just Israel or the church.

3. I Was Hungry

"for I was hungry and you gave me food, I was thirsty and you gave me something to drink, I was a stranger and you welcomed me, I was naked and you gave me clothing, I was sick and you took care of me, I was in prison and you visited me."

  • Six works: Christians later added "bury the dead" to make the seven corporal works of mercy.
  • Ordinary things: Nothing heroic: food, water, a visit.

4. When Did We See You?

"Lord, when was it that we saw you hungry and gave you food or thirsty and gave you something to drink?"

  • Surprised: The righteous didn't know.
  • So were the others: The ones on the left ask the same question.

5. One of the Least of These

"Truly I tell you, just as you did it to one of the least of these brothers and sisters of mine, you did it to me." Paul prays for "the eyes of your heart enlightened," and this is what those eyes see. The King isn't far off on a throne. He's at the door, at the bus stop, in the cell. On the last Sunday of the year, before Advent's waiting begins, the church crowns a king you meet in the least.

For Christians today

Christ the King is one of the newest feasts in the church year.

  • Its origin: Pope Pius XI created it in 1925, as nationalism and dictators rose in Europe, to say Christ, not any state, is king.
  • Catholic: Matthew 25 is the root of the corporal works of mercy, and of Mother Teresa's saying that she saw Christ in "his distressing disguise" among the poor.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many mainline churches adopted the feast with the Revised Common Lectionary; some prefer "Reign of Christ."
  • Episcopal: The Collect prays for the peoples of the earth, "divided and enslaved by sin," to be brought together under Christ.
  • Live debates today: Matthew 25 is cited constantly in debates over immigration, prisons and welfare; others argue "the least of these" first meant Jesus' persecuted followers.

Carry it today: Do one of the six works for someone this week.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.30, Long translation.) The most powerful king on earth wrote himself a warning not to become a "Caesar," puffed up by the purple. In the same passage he urges himself to stay simple, just and kind. On Christ the King Sunday, the Stoic emperor and the Gospel agree: real kingship looks like service.

The time. Ezekiel prophesied in Babylon, around 593–571 BC. Matthew was written around AD 80–90, likely for a mostly Jewish-Christian community.

What scholars broadly agree on. Matthew 25:31–46 is unique to Matthew and caps Jesus' final sermon on the end times (chapters 24–25).

What it meant then. Kings in the ancient world were judged by whether they protected widows, orphans and the poor; here the judge identifies with them.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw Christ the Good Shepherd of John 10 fulfilling Ezekiel 34; John Chrysostom preached that the poor are Christ's altar.

What's debated or unknown. Whether "the least of these brothers and sisters" means all needy people, or Christ's missionaries and disciples in particular.
