November 26th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Psalm 80 is a prayer from people who have lost a great deal. Three times they cry the same line: "Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved." Zechariah promises a fountain that will wash Jerusalem clean, and a remnant refined like silver who will call on God and be answered (paraphrased). Revelation shows an angel carrying good news to every nation on earth (paraphrased).

What it means: Thanksgiving isn't only for full years. Psalm 80 is gratitude's other half: asking God to turn his face toward us again. The shining face is the oldest blessing in the Bible, the priestly blessing asking God to make his face shine on you (Numbers 6:25, paraphrased). Today, the faces around the table may be how that blessing arrives.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: He taught that the wise person doesn't grieve over what he lacks but rejoices in what he has. (Fragments, paraphrased.)
  • Judaism: Psalm 80 is also in today's monthly cycle of Psalms, read by Jews around the world.
  • Bahá'í: Today is the Day of the Covenant, a feast of thanks for unity.
  • Sikhism: Guru Nanak taught that the Giver never tires of giving (paraphrased).

Carry it today: Whose face will you give thanks for today?

The theme

  • Shepherd of Israel: A plea to the one who leads.
  • Let your face shine: The refrain, three times.
  • The fountain: Cleansing promised.
  • Refined like silver: A remnant that calls and is answered.

Yesterday, Jeremiah gathered the flock home to a harvest feast. Today, the psalmist asks the Shepherd to look on them again.

1. Give Ear, O Shepherd

"Give ear, O Shepherd of Israel, you who lead Joseph like a flock! You who are enthroned upon the cherubim, shine forth."

  • Shepherd again: The same image as this week's readings in Ezekiel, Zechariah 11 and Jeremiah.
  • Enthroned on the cherubim: God's seat above the Ark of the Covenant.

2. Let Your Face Shine

"Restore us, O God; let your face shine, that we may be saved."

  • A refrain: It comes back in verses 3, 7 and 19, and grows each time: God, God of hosts, Lord God of hosts.
  • The priestly blessing: It echoes the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:25, where God's face shines on the people (paraphrased).

3. A Fountain Opened

Zechariah says that on that day a fountain will be opened for the house of David and Jerusalem, to wash away sin and impurity (paraphrased).

  • Cleansing: Before restoration comes washing.
  • Water again: Yesterday's watered garden becomes today's open fountain.

4. Refined Like Silver

Zechariah says God will refine the remnant like silver and test them like gold; they will call on his name and he will answer them (paraphrased).

  • Through fire: What's left after hardship is purer.
  • Call and answer: The relationship is restored: "You are my people," "The Lord is our God."

5. Rest From Their Labors

Revelation blesses those who die in the Lord, saying they rest from their labors and their deeds follow them (paraphrased). On a holiday, many tables have an empty chair. Thanksgiving can hold both gratitude for who's here and for those whose work is done.

For Christians today

Thanksgiving is a civic holiday with deep church roots.

  • Episcopal: The Book of Common Prayer has its own Thanksgiving Day collect and readings; many parishes hold a morning Eucharist.
  • Catholic: U.S. bishops provide Thanksgiving Day Mass texts, and today's Gospel ends, "lift up your heads."
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many families say a longer grace today, and churches serve community meals.
  • Orthodox: Today honors Gideon, the judge who asked God for a sign before he would act.
  • Eucharist: The word itself means "thanksgiving" in Greek.

Carry it today: Look around the table and silently bless each face.

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

Epictetus taught that a wise person does not grieve over the things he lacks, but rejoices in the things he has. (Fragments, paraphrased.) On Thanksgiving, that's the whole lesson. The Stoic practice is to look at what is here, the food, the people, this day, and let that be enough.

The time. Psalm 80 names the northern tribes (Ephraim, Benjamin and Manasseh), and many scholars link it to the fall of the northern kingdom to Assyria around 722 BC. Zechariah 9–14 is usually dated later than chapters 1–8, which come from about 520 BC. Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. Psalm 80 is a communal lament, likely sung in worship. Zechariah 9–14 differs in style from chapters 1–8 and is often called "Second Zechariah."

What it meant then. For a people overrun by empire, the prayer kept faith that God could still turn and save.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the vine of Psalm 80 as the church, and the shining face as Christ, the face of God made visible.

What's debated or unknown. The exact date and author of Zechariah 9–14, and whether Psalm 80 was first sung in the north or in Jerusalem.
