“What began as a gift can't be finished by effort. Keep asking; the Father keeps giving.”
October 8th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Yesterday Jesus taught his disciples the Lord's Prayer. Today he tells them a story about asking. A friend shows up at midnight hungry, and you have no bread, so you go knock on your neighbor's door. He's in bed with his kids and the door is barred. But if you keep knocking, he'll get up and give you what you need. Then Jesus says: ask, seek, knock. If you, who aren't perfect, give your children good things, how much more will the Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask? Meanwhile Paul is furious with the Galatians. They received the Spirit by trusting the good news, and now they think they have to earn it by keeping rules.

What it means: Both readings point at the same mistake: treating a gift like a wage. The Galatians started with grace and are trying to finish with effort. Jesus says the only "work" prayer needs is asking, and asking again. The neighbor isn't a picture of God, reluctant and grumpy. It's a "how much more" story: if even a sleepy neighbor gives in, how much more will a loving Father give. Persistence in prayer isn't wearing God down. It's how we stay open to receive.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Islam: the Qur'an has God say, in effect, "I am near, and I answer the one who calls" (2:186).
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna accepts even "a leaf, a flower, a fruit, water," if it's offered with love.
  • Pure Land Buddhism: Shinran taught that we're saved by Amida's "other-power," not our own effort.
  • Tao Te Ching: the follower of the Tao does less each day, not more.
  • Bahá'í: believers repeat the Báb's short prayer, "Is there any Remover of difficulties save God?", over and over.

Carry it today: What are you trying to earn that you only need to ask for?

The theme

  • A gift, not a wage: The Galatians received the Spirit by trusting. Paul's question is simple: why would you try to finish by effort what began as a gift?
  • Ask, seek, knock: Jesus's three verbs keep going (in Greek they mean "keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking"). Prayer isn't one request. It's a way of standing at the door.
  • How much more: The sleepy neighbor isn't God. He's the contrast. If even he gets up, how much more will a Father who loves you.
  • The answer is the Spirit: Jesus doesn't promise everything we ask for. He promises the Holy Spirit, which is what Paul says the Galatians already have.

Yesterday Jesus taught us what to pray. Today he teaches us how: boldly, repeatedly, and without thinking we have to earn the answer. And Paul shows what happens when people forget that.

1. A Knock at Midnight

In a first-century village, hospitality wasn't optional. A guest arriving at midnight was the whole village's honor at stake, and bread was baked each morning, so by night there might be none left.

  • The Barred Door: The neighbor's house is probably one room. The family sleeps together on a mat, the door bolted. Getting up means waking everyone. His "Don't bother me" is perfectly reasonable.
  • Shameless Asking: The Greek word in verse 8, anaideia, is usually translated "persistence," but literally means "shamelessness." Jesus praises the friend who isn't too proud or polite to keep knocking.

2. Keep Asking, Keep Seeking, Keep Knocking

The verbs in verse 9 are in a Greek tense that suggests ongoing action. Prayer isn't a vending machine you hit once.

  • Three Ways In: Asking is speaking our need. Seeking is looking for what we can't yet see. Knocking is standing at a closed door and refusing to walk away.
  • Everyone Who Asks: "For everyone who asks, receives." Not everyone who deserves, or everyone who prays well. Everyone who asks.

3. How Much More

Jesus finishes with a father and his child. No father hands his child a snake when they ask for a fish, or a scorpion when they ask for an egg.

  • Arguing from Small to Great: It's a classic Jewish form of argument (kal va-chomer, "light and heavy"): if this is true of a flawed human father, how much more of God.
  • The Gift Is the Spirit: Matthew's version says the Father gives "good things." Luke changes it to "the Holy Spirit." The best answer to prayer is God's own presence.

4. "O Foolish Galatians!"

Paul is at his most heated. Some teachers have told the non-Jewish believers in Galatia that faith in Christ isn't enough: they must also be circumcised and keep the Law.

  • "Who Has Bewitched You?": Paul uses a word tied to the "evil eye," as if someone had cast a spell on them.
  • Begun in the Spirit: "Having started with the Spirit, are you now ending with the flesh?" For Paul, "flesh" here means our own effort and achievement. The Galatians had already received the Spirit, freely, when they trusted. Rules can't add to that.

5. The Ego Wants to Earn It

Put the readings together and the trap becomes clear. The ego loves earning. It feels safer to deserve something than to receive it, because receiving means admitting we need. The Galatians drifted from gift to performance. We do the same, in prayer and in life. Jesus's answer is humble and almost childlike: keep asking. Asking is how the ego lets go. The door that opens isn't a reward. It was always meant to open.

For Christians today

Ask, seek, knock is one of the best-loved promises in the Bible, and Galatians 3 is a key text in the oldest Christian argument: grace versus works.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine preached on the friend at midnight and read it as a call to keep asking even when God seems to delay. He held that God sometimes waits so that our desire will grow, making room for a bigger gift. The early church saw "ask, and it will be given" as a promise about spiritual gifts first, not material ones.
  • Catholic: Catholic teaching holds that grace is always God's free gift, and that our cooperation is itself made possible by grace. The Catechism (§2613) lists the friend at midnight among Luke's parables on prayer and draws from it the lesson of persistent, confident prayer.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Galatians was Luther's favorite letter; he lectured on it and wrote a famous commentary. "Justified by faith, not works" became the Reformation's banner, and Galatians 3:1–5 is a classic text for it. Evangelicals often stress Luke 11:13 in teaching on the Holy Spirit.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church speaks of prayer as a lifelong path of theosis, becoming united with God by grace. Saint Seraphim of Sarov taught that the true aim of the Christian life is "the acquisition of the Holy Spirit," which is exactly what Luke 11:13 promises.
  • Live debates today: Christians still argue about "unanswered" prayer and the "prosperity gospel," which treats ask-and-receive as a promise of wealth and health. Most mainstream teachers answer from Luke's own wording: the promised gift is the Holy Spirit, not everything on our wish list. The 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between Catholics and Lutherans tried to narrow the grace-versus-works divide that Galatians started.

Carry it today: Pick one thing you've stopped praying about because it seemed hopeless, and knock on that door one more time.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: offering and grace.

  • The Hindu lens: In bhakti, the path of devotion, the smallest offering given with love is enough (Gita 9.26). Some later schools argued over grace much as Paul did: the "monkey" school said the soul clings to God like a baby monkey to its mother, by its own effort, and the "cat" school said God carries the soul like a kitten in its mother's mouth, by grace alone.
  • The Jain lens: Jainism takes the opposite path from Paul: liberation comes only through one's own effort, with no God to give it. Yet Jains also teach that the effort is mostly letting go, of possessions, anger and pride.

Today is Krishna Trayodashi, the thirteenth day of the dark half, with the moon in Purva Phalguni (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). It's Trayodashi Shraddha in Pitru Paksha, and the evening is Pradosh, a twilight fast and worship of Shiva on the thirteenth day of each lunar half (called Guru Pradosh when it falls on Thursday). What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Trayodashi: trayas, "three" (compare Latin tres), plus daśa, "ten" (compare Latin decem): the thirteenth lunar day.
  • Pradosh: pradoṣa, "early night, twilight," the hour of the Shiva worship.
  • Shraddha: from śraddhā, "faith, trust" (from the same root as Latin credo, "I believe"). The rites for the ancestors are named for faith, a good word on a day about faith, not works.
  • Bhakti: from bhaj, "to share in, to love": devotion.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A Greek philosophy founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its best-known voices are Epictetus, once a slave; Seneca, adviser to Nero; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. Its core idea is to tell apart what is up to us (our judgments, choices and actions) from what isn't, and to give ourselves fully to the first. Its four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"God is near you, he is with you, he is within you. This is what I mean, Lucilius: a holy spirit indwells within us, one who marks our good and bad deeds, and is our guardian." (Seneca, Letter 41, Gummere translation.) Seneca told his friend there was no need to lift hands to heaven or beg a temple-keeper to let him near an idol's ear. Paul would have agreed about the Spirit within, and Jesus adds that the Father still loves to be asked.

The time. Paul wrote Galatians around AD 50–55, about twenty years after Jesus's death, to churches he had founded in Galatia, a Roman province in central Asia Minor (modern Turkey). Luke wrote his Gospel around AD 80–90. The parable is set in the villages of Galilee and Judea under Roman rule, where most families lived in small one-room houses and baked bread daily.

What scholars broadly agree on. Nearly all scholars accept Galatians as genuinely written by Paul, one of his most personal letters. They agree the conflict was real: other teachers were urging non-Jewish believers to be circumcised and keep the Jewish Law. The friend at midnight is found only in Luke, while "ask, seek, knock" also appears in Matthew 7:7–11, so most scholars think both drew on a shared collection of Jesus's sayings. They also agree that Luke changed "good things" to "the Holy Spirit," in line with his focus on the Spirit throughout Luke and Acts.

What it meant then. For Jesus's first hearers, the story was about the honor of hospitality. No villager would refuse a neighbor bread for a guest; the shame would be unthinkable. So the point is: if even a reluctant neighbor won't refuse, God surely won't. For Paul, the point was who belongs. If non-Jews had to keep the Law to be full members, then faith in Christ wasn't enough. Paul argued their experience of the Spirit proved it was.

The allegorical readings. Augustine, preaching on this passage, read the three loaves as faith in the Trinity, and the fish, egg and bread as faith, hope and love. Others read the friend arriving at midnight as the soul in need, and the closed door as the times when God seems silent. These are interpretations, not the passage's plain meaning.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate where exactly the "Galatians" lived: in the north of the province among ethnic Gauls, or in southern cities like Lystra and Derbe that Paul visited in Acts. That affects the letter's date. The Greek word anaideia in Luke 11:8 is also debated: does it describe the asker's persistence, or the sleeping neighbor's wish to avoid shame? Some manuscripts of Luke 11 add a line about bread and a stone, borrowed from Matthew.
