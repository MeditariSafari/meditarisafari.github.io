The theme

To get past a surface-level, transactional interpretation of today's readings (October 4, 2026), we have to look at the psychological and spiritual mechanics beneath the metaphors.

When you look closely at Matthew 21:33–46 (The Parable of the Wicked Tenants) paired with Isaiah 5:1–7 (The Song of the Vineyard), the readings are not actually a historical scolding of ancient religious leaders. They are a profound diagnostic on ego, possession, and the subtle ways human consciousness slips into spiritual delusion.

1. The Delusion of Ownership vs. Stewardship

In the Gospel, the landowner builds a gorgeous, fully functioning vineyard—complete with a protective hedge, a wine press, and a watchtower—and then leaves it in the care of tenants.

The "intelligent interpretation" here isn't just that the tenants were bad guys. It is an exploration of a universal human flaw: conflating stewardship with ownership.

  • The Ego's Hijacking: The tenants didn't build the vineyard. They didn't dig the wine press. Yet, over time, the physical proximity to the blessing tricked their egos into believing they were the masters of it.
  • The Modern Equivalent: This is exactly what happens when we treat our lives, our talents, our intellect, or our spiritual wisdom as personal property. The moment we believe "I built this, this is mine," we stop treating our life as a gift to be offered back to the world. We become highly defensive, greedy, and territorial.

2. The Murder of the Messengers (The Echo Chamber of Preconceived Notions)

When the owner sends servants to collect the fruit, the tenants beat and kill them. Finally, they murder the owner's son, thinking, "If we kill the heir, the inheritance will be ours".

Logically, their plan makes no sense. Killing a king's son doesn't make you the king; it brings an army to your door. But spiritually and psychologically, this perfectly captures the insanity of the ego when its dominance is threatened.

  • Silencing the Truth: The "servants" and the "son" represent reality breaking through our delusions. When someone or something challenges our self-righteousness, our immediate instinct is to destroy the messenger.
  • Spiritual Failure Through Being Right: The chief priests and elders in the story were the ultimate religious experts. They knew the law inside and out. Yet, their intense need to maintain their position and be right made them totally blind to the living truth standing right in front of them. It is a stark warning that academic or traditional religious certainty can actually be the ultimate barrier to genuine spiritual awakening.

3. Isaiah's Vineyard: When Good Intentions Yield Sour Fruits

In Isaiah 5, God does everything right, but the vineyard yields "wild, sour grapes" instead of sweet ones. Isaiah beautifully summarizes the tragedy: God looked for mishpat (justice) but saw mishpach (bloodshed); He looked for tsedakah (righteousness) but heard tseakah (a cry of distress).

  • The Illusion of Effort: This text targets our internal spiritual state. You can do all the external mechanics right—attend church, read books, memorize scripture, construct the perfect "watchtower" of moral behavior—but if the underlying heart hasn't shifted from bitterness to love, the fruit of your life will still taste sour to those around you.
  • The Metric of True Spirituality: The text forces us to ask: What does the harvest of our daily lives actually taste like to our family, our coworkers, and our neighbors? Is it sweet with empathy and presence, or is it sour with judgment and a demand for control?

4. The Cornerstone and the Shift in Consciousness

Jesus caps off the parable by quoting Psalm 118: "The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone".

The "builders" are the elite architects of society and religion. They look at a specific stone—one that doesn't fit into their systems of power, exclusion, or egoic dominance—and throw it away. But that rejected, humble space of total surrender and unconditional love is precisely where God builds reality.

Today's scripture is a powerful call to a massive paradigm shift. It tells us that the Kingdom of God isn't a country club for people who have the right opinions. It is a living, breathing field of consciousness given exclusively to "a people that will produce its fruit"—those who have dropped the illusion of control, stopped fighting to prove they are right, and simply allowed themselves to become a conduit for divine love.

What's an uposatha day? The word is Pali, from Sanskrit upavasatha: upa, "near," plus vas, "to dwell" or "to fast" (dwelling close to the teaching, or keeping the fast). It's the Buddhist observance day, held four times a month on the new moon, full moon and the two half moons. Lay Buddhists keep eight precepts for the day instead of the usual five (no food after noon, no entertainment, no luxury, celibacy), often visit a monastery to hear teaching and meditate, and monks gather on the new and full moons to recite their rule together. It's roughly the Buddhist sabbath.

Today is an uposatha day, the waning half moon. Next one: the new moon on October 11. The rains retreat, three months when monks stay put to practice, ends with Pavāraṇā on October 26.

What is Stoicism? A school of philosophy founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught, and later practiced by Romans like the slave Epictetus, the statesman Seneca and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. Its core idea is simple: some things are up to us (our judgments, choices and actions) and most things aren't (other people, health, reputation, events). Peace comes from putting all our effort into the first and accepting the second. Stoics aimed to live by reason and four virtues (wisdom, courage, justice and self-control), saw every person as part of one human family, and kept daily practices like morning preparation and evening review. Early Christians borrowed a lot from them.

"…he is like a vine which has produced grapes, and seeks for nothing more after it has once produced its proper fruit… a man when he has done a good act, does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act, as a vine goes on to produce again the grapes in season." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.6, Long translation) Marcus picked the same picture as Isaiah and Jesus. The vine doesn't hoard its grapes or ask for applause. It bears fruit and gets ready to bear more.

Today is Krishna Navami until 5:23 PM, then Dashami. The moon is in Punarvasu, moving into Pushya at 1:43 PM. Today is also Navami Shraddha in Pitru Paksha, the day many families honor their mothers and the women of the family who have died. Sunday belongs to Surya.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Krishna Navami: kṛṣṇa means "dark" or "black," which is also the god Krishna's name (he's pictured dark-skinned). Here it means the dark half of the month, when the moon is waning. Navami is from nava, "nine" (the same Indo-European root as Latin novem and English nine). So it's "the ninth day of the dark half."
  • Dashami: from daśa, "ten" (compare Latin decem). The tenth day.
  • Paksha: literally "wing" or "side." A lunar month has two wings: the bright, waxing half (shukla, "white") and the dark, waning half (krishna).
  • Tithi: a "lunar day," the time the moon takes to gain 12° on the sun. That's why a tithi can start and end mid-afternoon instead of at midnight.
  • Nakshatra: a "lunar mansion," one of 27 star groups the moon passes through, about one a day.
  • Punarvasu: punar, "again," plus vasu, "good" or "wealth." It means "good again" or "the return of the light," and it's linked to Rama coming home from exile.
  • Pushya: from the root puṣ, "to nourish, to thrive." It means "the nourishing one," and it's considered one of the most auspicious nakshatras.
  • Pitru Paksha: pitṛ, "father" or "ancestor" (the same root as Latin pater and English father), plus paksha. It means "the fortnight of the ancestors."
  • Shraddha: from śraddhā, "faith" or "trust." The rite is named for the spirit it's done in: offerings of food and water to the dead, made in faith.
  • Surya: "the sun," the sun god honored on Sundays.

1Richard Rohr / CAC: "God's Resting Place"(week theme: Francis and the Natural World)On Francis's feast, Rohr says creation itself was Francis's "primary cathedral, his resting place in God."

Drawing on Francis's early biographers, Thomas of Celano and Bonaventure, Rohr describes how Francis spoke to animals and the elements as if they had souls, because for him they carried God's presence. He notes how much of Jesus's own teaching came from watching nature: a red sky, lilies, birds, grass, a fig tree. Rohr's point is that creation is a constant, free source of spiritual nourishment, and that long stretches outdoors often open people up more than formal religious practice does. It lands right beside today's readings, where God's message comes through a vineyard.

  • Tao Te Ching: "All things spring up… they grow, and there is no claim made for their ownership; they go through their processes, and there is no expectation (of a reward for the results). The work is accomplished, and there is no resting in it (as an achievement)." (Ch. 2, Legge translation.) The Tao bears fruit and claims none of it, the exact opposite of the tenants.

  • Gurdjieff: He taught that real growth comes through "conscious labor and intentional suffering": choosing effort and accepting discomfort on purpose, rather than drifting. (Paraphrased.) It's AA's pruning in his own vocabulary: the cutting-back is chosen, and it's what lets something real grow.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: He taught "interbeing": look closely at a sheet of paper and you'll see the cloud, the rain, the tree and the logger in it, because nothing exists on its own.   • Ram Dass: In How Can I Help?, written with Paul Gorman, Ram Dass explored how to serve without turning it into being "the helper," with the ego that comes with it.   • Buddha: "As the bee collects nectar and departs without injuring the flower, or its colour or scent, so let a sage dwell in his village." (Dhammapada 49, Müller translation.) Take what you need from the world and leave it whole, which is how Francis lived with creation.
