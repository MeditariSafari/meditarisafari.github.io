November 18th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Esther is a Jewish girl who became queen of Persia without telling anyone who her people were. Then the king's chief minister, Haman, got a decree to destroy every Jew in the empire. Her cousin Mordecai told her she could not stay silent. Today she speaks. At a private banquet she asks the king for her own life and her people's lives. Who would do such a thing? she's asked. "A foe and an enemy, this wicked Haman!" Haman is terrified. By evening he is dead on the pole he had set up for Mordecai. In the Gospel, Jesus praises the servant who simply keeps doing his job: "Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives."

What it means: Esther had every reason to stay hidden. Speaking meant risking her life. But silence would have meant her people's deaths. Evil often depends on good people keeping quiet, and sometimes it falls into the trap it set for others.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Some things are in our control and others not." (Enchiridion 1.) Esther couldn't control the king; she could control whether she spoke.
  • Judaism: This is the Purim story, read aloud every spring; the crowd drowns out Haman's name with noise.
  • Buddha: Right speech means speaking what is true and helpful, at the right time (paraphrased).
  • Sikhism: Guru Tegh Bahadur gave his life to defend the freedom of others to worship.

Carry it today: What truth do you need to speak out loud?

The theme

  • Esther speaks: Breaking her silence.
  • She names the enemy: "This wicked Haman!"
  • The trap closes: On the one who built it.
  • The faithful servant: Found at work.

Yesterday, Pharaoh's daughter saved one Hebrew child. Today, a hidden Jewish queen saves a whole people.

1. Let My Life Be Given Me

"If I have won your favor, O king, and if it pleases the king, let my life be given me—that is my petition."

  • Risking everything: Anyone who came to the king uninvited could be put to death (Esther 4:11). Esther went anyway.
  • "For such a time as this": Mordecai had told her she might have become queen for exactly this moment (Esther 4:14).

2. A Foe and an Enemy

"Esther said, 'A foe and an enemy, this wicked Haman!' Then Haman was terrified before the king and the queen."

  • Naming it: Esther doesn't hint. She names the man in the room.
  • The tables turn: The powerful man is suddenly the frightened one.

3. The Pole He Prepared

"So they hung Haman on the pole that he had prepared for Mordecai. Then the anger of the king abated."

  • Poetic justice: Haman built a pole about 75 feet high for Mordecai (Esther 5:14).
  • God unnamed: The Book of Esther never mentions God, yet the story is full of reversals faith reads as providence.

4. Found at Work

"Blessed is that slave whom his master will find at work when he arrives."

  • Faithful in the meantime: The good servant keeps feeding the household.
  • The wicked servant: He beats his fellow servants, thinking no one will ever check.

5. The Psalm's Cry

Psalm 83 begins by asking God not to keep silent while enemies plot to erase Israel's name. Esther is how that prayer got answered: not by a voice from heaven, but by a woman who refused to keep silent herself. Like the faithful servant, she was found at work when the moment came.

For Christians today

Christians have often found courage in Esther.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christian writers saw Esther interceding with the king as a figure of Mary, or of the church, pleading for the people.
  • Catholic: Rose Philippine Duchesne, today's U.S. saint, waited decades to serve among Native Americans and finally went at 71; the Potawatomi called her "Woman Who Prays Always."
  • Protestant and evangelical: "For such a time as this" is one of the most quoted lines in sermons on calling and courage.
  • Episcopal: Hilda of Whitby, today's commemoration, led a great monastery of men and women and hosted the Synod of Whitby in 664.
  • Live debates today: Esther is often cited in debates about when believers must speak out against injustice, and about women's leadership.

Carry it today: Say one true thing you've been keeping quiet.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Some things are in our control and others not." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 1, Higginson translation.) Esther couldn't control the king's mood, Haman's plot, or whether she'd live. She could control one thing: whether she spoke. Epictetus, a former slave, knew that is where freedom lives.

The time. Esther is set in Susa, the Persian capital, under King Ahasuerus, usually identified as Xerxes I (reigned 486–465 BC). Matthew was written around AD 80–90.

What scholars broadly agree on. Most scholars read Esther as a historical novella rather than a court record; there's no Persian record of Queen Esther, and Xerxes' known queen was Amestris. Its details of Persian court life are often accurate.

What it meant then. For Jews scattered across the Persian empire, Esther showed that a people far from home and Temple could survive by courage and solidarity.

The allegorical readings. Rabbis noted that God's name is hidden in Esther, just as God seemed hidden in exile; the name Esther itself sounds like the Hebrew for "I will hide."

What's debated or unknown. Whether any of it happened, and why the Greek version adds prayers and mentions God while the Hebrew never does.
