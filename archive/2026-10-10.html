“You don't belong to God's family by birth or rank. You belong by hearing the word and living it.”
October 10th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jesus is teaching a crowd when a woman shouts out a compliment, the way people did back then: "Blessed is the mother who gave birth to you and nursed you!" Jesus doesn't brush his mother off, but he turns the blessing outward: "Rather, blessed are those who hear the word of God and keep it." In Galatians, Paul says the Law was like a babysitter until faith came. Now, through baptism, everyone has "put on Christ" like a new set of clothes. There's no more Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female. All are one.

What it means: People in Jesus's day measured worth by family, tribe, gender and status. Jesus and Paul both cut through that. Your place in God's family doesn't come from who your mother is, where you were born, or what rank you hold. It comes from hearing God's word and living it. That opens the door to everyone, and it takes away everyone's excuse. Even Mary, the Church has always said, was blessed most of all because she heard the word and kept it.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Buddha: "I do not call a man a Brâhmana because of his origin or of his mother."
  • Confucius: live well and respectfully, and "all within the four seas will be his brothers."
  • Marcus Aurelius: as a Roman his city is Rome, but "so far as I am a man, it is the world."
  • Kahlil Gibran: "Your children are not your children."
  • Martin Luther King Jr.: we are all "tied in a single garment of destiny."

Carry it today: What label do you lean on for your worth, and what would it mean to set it down?

The theme

  • Hearing beats pedigree: Jesus moves the blessing from his mother's body to anyone who hears and keeps God's word. Belonging is about response, not birth.
  • Mary heard first: This isn't a put-down of Mary. Luke shows her as the model listener, the one who said "let it be done to me according to your word."
  • One new outfit: In baptism we're "clothed with Christ." The old labels of race, class and gender no longer decide who's in.
  • Children, not servants: Paul says the Law was a guardian for children. Faith makes us grown sons and daughters, heirs with full rights.

Yesterday the house had to be filled. Today we learn who gets to live in it: everyone who hears and keeps the word.

1. A Shout from the Crowd

"Blessed is the womb that carried you and the breasts at which you nursed." It sounds strange to us, but it was a common way to praise a remarkable son: honor the mother who raised him.

  • A Real Compliment: The woman means well. She's moved by Jesus and says the most natural thing she can think of.
  • Jesus Redirects: "Rather" (the Greek word menoun can mean "yes, but more than that"). He doesn't deny his mother's blessing. He points to a deeper one that's open to everyone.

2. Hear and Keep

Jesus's blessing has two parts: hearing God's word and keeping it.

  • Hearing: Real listening, the kind that lets a word in, not just noise passing by.
  • Keeping: The Greek phylassō means "to guard," like a watchman. We keep the word by protecting it and living by it. It ties back to the swept house: the word is what fills it.

3. Mary, the First Disciple

Luke has already shown us Mary as the perfect listener. At the Annunciation she said, "May it be done to me according to your word." At Jesus's birth she "kept all these things, reflecting on them in her heart."

  • Not a Snub: Augustine said Mary was more blessed for receiving Christ by faith than for conceiving him in the flesh. Jesus's answer actually honors her most of all.
  • Open to All: What made Mary blessed is something anyone can share.

4. Clothed in Christ

Paul says the Law was a paidagogos, a household servant who walked children to school and kept them in line until they grew up. Now faith has come, and we're no longer under a guardian.

  • New Clothes: "All of you who were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ." In the ancient world, clothing showed your rank. Paul says everyone now wears the same garment.
  • Neither, Nor, And: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free person, there is not male and female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." The three great divisions of the ancient world, ethnicity, class and gender, no longer decide who belongs.

5. Belonging Without Labels

Put the readings together and the message is freeing and demanding at once. Freeing, because no one is shut out by their birth, race, class or gender. Demanding, because no one gets in by those things either. The ego loves labels: my family, my people, my rank. Jesus and Paul ask us to take them off and put on one new garment. What counts is whether we hear God's word and live it.

For Christians today

Galatians 3:28 is one of the most influential verses in Christian history, and Luke 11:27–28 is central to how Christians think about Mary.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine wrote that Mary was more blessed in receiving the faith of Christ than in conceiving his flesh, so Jesus's words in Luke 11 honor her above all. Early baptisms often clothed the newly baptized in white robes, acting out Paul's "clothed yourselves with Christ."
  • Catholic: Vatican II called Mary the model of the Church because she heard the word of God and kept it (Lumen Gentium, ch. 8). Catholics see Luke 11:28 not as a rebuke but as the deepest reason for her blessedness, which is why it's read on Marian feasts.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Protestants often read Luke 11:27–28 as a caution against honoring Mary too much, with the stress on hearing and obeying the word. Galatians 3:28 has been a key verse in debates over slavery, race and the role of women in church leadership.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church calls Mary Theotokos, "God-bearer," and honors her above all saints, while teaching that her holiness came from her free and faithful "yes" to God. Many Orthodox baptisms still clothe the newly baptized in a white garment while the choir sings "As many as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27).
  • Live debates today: American abolitionists quoted Galatians 3:28 against slavery, while some defenders of slavery argued it applied only to spiritual equality. Today Christians still argue whether "neither male nor female" bears on women's ordination, and how the verse should shape churches divided by race and class.

Carry it today: Notice one person you mentally file under a label today, and treat them simply as family.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: honoring every ancestor.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Mahalaya Amavasya (Sarva Pitru Amavasya), the last and greatest day of Pitru Paksha, when families offer rites for all ancestors, including those whose death dates are unknown. No one is left out. In Bengal, Mahalaya morning also welcomes the goddess Durga with the dawn recitation of Mahishasura Mardini. The Gita teaches that God is "alike for all" (9.29).
  • The Jain lens: The Jain tradition rejected caste from early on: Mahavira accepted followers from every caste, and Jain texts teach that a person becomes noble by conduct, not by birth. Women were admitted as nuns from the beginning, and the Jain order has long had more nuns than monks.

Today is Amavasya, the new moon that closes the dark half, with the moon in Hasta (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Sharad Navaratri, nine nights honoring the Goddess, begins tomorrow, October 11. Saturday belongs to Shani (Saturn) and to Hanuman.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Amavasya: amā, "together," plus vas, "to dwell": the night the sun and moon "dwell together," the new moon.
  • Sarva Pitru: sarva, "all," plus pitṛ, "ancestor, father" (same root as Latin pater): "all the ancestors."
  • Mahalaya: mahā, "great," plus ālaya, "abode": "the great abode."
  • Durga: "the hard to reach" or "the invincible," from dur-, "hard," plus ga, "going."
  • Hasta: "hand" (the nakshatra pictured as an open hand).

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, who taught at the stoa poikile, the painted porch. Its great teachers include Epictetus, a former slave; Seneca, a wealthy statesman; and Marcus Aurelius, an emperor, which shows how little rank mattered to it. It teaches us to focus on what is up to us (our judgments, choices and actions) and to accept what isn't. Its four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control, and it saw all people as citizens of one world.

"If a man should be able to assent to this doctrine as he ought, that we are all sprung from God in an especial manner, and that God is the father both of men and of gods, I suppose that he would never have any ignoble or mean thoughts about himself." (Epictetus, Discourses 1.3, Long translation.) Epictetus, who had been enslaved, taught that our true dignity comes from being children of God, not from rank. Paul says much the same: through faith, "you are all children of God."

  • Women and men: Guru Nanak asked why anyone should call woman inferior, since she gives birth to kings (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 473, paraphrased). It's a striking answer to a day that begins with a blessing on a mother.

Hukamnama (the verse drawn each morning from the Guru Granth Sahib at the Golden Temple and read by Sikhs worldwide): Ang 685 (ang, "limb," the reverent word for a page), Raag Dhanaasree, Guru Nanak Dev Ji. The Guru is pictured as an ocean full of pearls: whoever dives in finds the treasure, whatever their birth.

The time. Paul wrote Galatians around AD 50–55. Luke wrote his Gospel around AD 80–90. In the Roman world, social rank was everything: ethnic identity, whether one was enslaved or free, and gender decided legal rights, seating at meals, and even what clothes one could wear. Perhaps a third of the people in some Roman cities were enslaved.

What scholars broadly agree on. Many scholars think Galatians 3:28 quotes an early baptismal formula that Paul and his churches already used, since similar lists appear in 1 Corinthians 12:13 and Colossians 3:11. The phrase "male and female" breaks the "neither/nor" pattern and echoes Genesis 1:27 ("male and female he created them") word for word in Greek, which suggests a deliberate allusion to creation. The paidagogos was a well-known figure, an enslaved household attendant who escorted boys to school and disciplined them. Luke 11:27–28 is found only in Luke, but its point matches Luke 8:21 and Mark 3:31–35.

What it meant then. For the Galatians, Paul's point was that non-Jewish believers didn't need to become Jewish to be full members: baptism, not circumcision, marked who belonged. For Luke's readers, the woman's blessing was a familiar compliment, and Jesus's answer made discipleship, not family ties, the real bond of his community.

The allegorical readings. The church fathers read "clothed with Christ" in light of Adam and Eve, who lost their garment of glory and were clothed in skins; baptism restores the robe. Some read the woman in the crowd as a figure of the Church, praising Mary as she still does. Others saw the paidagogos as the whole Old Testament, leading humanity like a child toward Christ. These are interpretations, not the passage's plain meaning.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate how far Paul meant Galatians 3:28 to change social life, since elsewhere he tells enslaved people and women to keep their places, though his letter to Philemon asks him to receive his slave Onesimus "as a beloved brother." Whether Galatians 3:28 began as a baptismal formula is likely but not certain. The Greek menoun in Luke 11:28 can mean "rather" or "yes, indeed," so scholars disagree on whether Jesus is correcting the woman or building on her blessing.
