“God sets up six cities of refuge where anyone who has killed by accident can be safe until a fair trial. Jesus warns of hard times and calls his followers to endure without letting love grow cold.”
November 7th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: In the ancient world, if someone killed a person, even by accident, the victim's family had the right to take revenge. God tells Joshua to set aside six cities of refuge, spread across the land so one is always within reach. Anyone who has killed by accident can run there, stand at the city gate, and be taken in and protected until a fair trial. The cities are open "for all the Israelites and for the aliens residing among them." In Matthew, Jesus looks at the great Temple and says it will be torn down. Hard times are coming, he warns: wars, deception, and love growing cold. But "the one who endures to the end will be saved."

What it means: God built mercy into the law. In a world ruled by revenge, the cities of refuge said: there must be a safe place, and a fair hearing, before anyone pays with their life. The question for us is whether we can be that kind of place for others: somewhere a person who has made a terrible mistake can run to, and find safety instead of vengeance. And when hard times come, Jesus asks us not to let our love grow cold.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer." (Meditations 6.6, Long.)
  • Buddha: He taught that hatred is never ended by hatred, but only by love (Dhammapada 5, paraphrased).
  • Judaism: The Talmud teaches that roads to the cities of refuge were kept wide and marked with signs saying "Refuge" (Makkot 10b, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Qur'an teaches that the recompense of an evil is an evil like it, but whoever pardons and makes peace, their reward is with God (42:40, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Who needs you to be a place of refuge?

The theme

  • Cities of refuge: Six safe towns spread across the land.
  • At the gate: The one who flees tells their story and is taken in.
  • For the alien too: Refuge is open to foreigners as well as Israelites.
  • Love grown cold: Jesus warns that hard times can freeze the heart.

This week Israel crossed the Jordan, took Jericho, and renewed the covenant. Today, the law builds in mercy: places of refuge for those who need them.

1. Appoint the Cities of Refuge

"Say to the Israelites: Appoint the cities of refuge, of which I spoke to you through Moses."

  • Promised long before: Moses had commanded these cities (Numbers 35; Deuteronomy 19). Now they become real places.
  • Within reach: Three on each side of the Jordan, so no one is too far from safety.

2. Without Intent or By Mistake

"So that anyone who kills a person without intent or by mistake may flee there; they shall be for you a refuge from the avenger of blood."

  • Intent matters: The law distinguishes accident from murder. Justice looks at the heart.
  • Breaking the cycle: Blood feuds could go on for generations. Refuge stops the spiral.

3. At the Entrance of the Gate

"The slayer shall flee to one of these cities and shall stand at the entrance of the gate of the city," and explain the case to the elders, who take them in.

  • Telling the truth: Refuge begins with honest confession at the gate.
  • Taken in: The elders give the person a place to live among them.

4. For the Aliens Too

"These were the cities designated for all the Israelites and for the aliens residing among them."

  • No borders on mercy: Foreigners have the same right to refuge.
  • Until a trial: Refuge isn't escape from justice; it's protection until justice can be fair.

5. Love Must Not Grow Cold

Jesus, leaving the Temple, foresees a time of upheaval: "Because of the increase of lawlessness, the love of many will grow cold. But the one who endures to the end will be saved." The two readings meet here. In a violent world, God made room for mercy. In the hardest times, Jesus asks his followers to keep their love warm. Christians have long seen Christ himself as our city of refuge, and Hebrews speaks of believers taking refuge in God's promise (6:18). The church at its best becomes a city of refuge: a place where people who have done wrong, or been wronged, can run and be safe.

For Christians today

The cities of refuge have shaped Christian thinking on mercy, justice and sanctuary.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers saw the cities of refuge as a figure of Christ, to whom sinners flee for safety. Hebrews 6:18 uses the language of fleeing for refuge.
  • Sanctuary: In medieval Europe, churches could offer sanctuary to fugitives, an idea rooted partly in the cities of refuge. Some churches today offer sanctuary to immigrants facing deportation.
  • Catholic: Catholic teaching on restorative justice and opposition to the death penalty draws on the idea that justice must leave room for mercy.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many churches run ministries for people leaving prison, addiction or abuse, often described as places of refuge.
  • Live debates today: How churches should balance sanctuary, law and justice is a live and debated question.

Carry it today: Offer someone who has failed a safe place instead of judgment.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.6, Long translation.) One sentence from an emperor who had every power to take revenge. The cities of refuge broke the cycle of vengeance by law. Marcus breaks it inside: refuse to become the one who wronged you.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; Matthew wrote around AD 80–90, after the Temple's destruction in AD 70.

What scholars broadly agree on. The cities of refuge reflect an effort to limit blood vengeance by giving accused killers asylum and a hearing. Similar asylum practices existed at altars and shrines in the ancient world (1 Kings 1:50). Matthew 24 reflects the trauma of the Temple's fall.

What it meant then. For Israel, the cities limited revenge and made justice more fair. For Matthew's community, Jesus' words helped them face the loss of the Temple and persecution.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the six cities as Christ, the refuge of sinners.

What's debated or unknown. Whether the cities of refuge functioned in practice, or mainly as an ideal, is debated.
