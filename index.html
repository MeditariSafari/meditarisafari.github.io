November 16th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Deborah gives Barak God's command, and Barak hesitates: "If you will go with me, I will go, but if you will not go with me, I will not go." Deborah goes. But she warns him: the glory won't be his, "for the Lord will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman." At Mount Tabor, God throws Sisera's army into panic. Sisera jumps from his chariot and runs to the tent of Jael, who seems to welcome him, gives him milk and covers him with a rug. While he sleeps, she kills him. In Romans, Paul says God's kindness is meant to lead us to change, and "God shows no partiality."

What it means: Barak isn't a coward; he knows he needs Deborah's faith beside him. Sometimes courage is borrowed, and that's no shame. But the story also overturns every expectation: the great general dies at the hands of a woman in a tent. God works through the people no one expects.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Be not ashamed to be helped; for it is thy business to do thy duty like a soldier in the assault on a town." (Meditations 7.7, Long.)
  • Judaism: The rabbis praised Jael, and the Song of Deborah calls her "most blessed of women."
  • Buddha: He called good friendship the whole of the spiritual life (Samyutta Nikaya 45.2, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Qur'an says, help one another in goodness and piety (5:2, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Whose courage do you lean on, and who leans on yours?

Yesterday Deborah called Barak. Today we see what happened next, and why the parable of the talents' warning against fear fits so well.

1. If You Will Go With Me

Barak said to her, "If you will go with me, I will go, but if you will not go with me, I will not go."

  • Weakness or wisdom? Some readers see doubt; others see a man who knew he needed the prophet's presence.
  • Hebrews 11:32: Barak is listed among the heroes of faith.

2. Not to Your Glory

"I will surely go with you; nevertheless, the road on which you are going will not lead to your glory, for the Lord will sell Sisera into the hand of a woman."

  • A twist: Readers expect the woman to be Deborah. It turns out to be Jael.
  • Glory belongs to God: No human hero takes the credit.

3. This Is the Day

"Up! For this is the day on which the Lord has given Sisera into your hand."

  • Deborah's courage: She gives the signal.
  • The storm: The Song of Deborah tells how the stars fought from heaven and the river Kishon swept the enemy away (Judges 5:20–21, paraphrased).

4. Jael's Tent

"But Jael wife of Heber took a tent peg and took a hammer in her hand and went softly to him and drove the peg into his temple."

  • A shocking story: Ancient hospitality made a guest sacred, and Jael breaks the rule to save Israel.
  • An outsider: Jael was a Kenite, not an Israelite.

5. God Shows No Partiality

"So on that day God subdued King Jabin of Canaan before the Israelites." Paul's word today fits the whole story: "God shows no partiality." Not to generals, not to men, not to insiders. A hesitant commander, a prophet under a palm, and an outsider woman in a tent all have a part. And none of them wins alone.

For Christians today

The story of Barak, Deborah and Jael raises hard questions and offers real comfort.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read Jael's victory as the church defeating evil, though they wrestled with her violence.
  • Catholic: Today's saints, Margaret of Scotland and Gertrude the Great, were strong women of faith: one a queen who fed the poor daily, the other a mystic of the heart of Christ.
  • Orthodox: Today's apostle, Matthew, was a tax collector, an outsider whom Jesus called anyway (Matthew 9:9).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Barak's "if you go with me" is often read as a lesson in the need for spiritual partners.
  • Live debates today: The violence of Judges troubles many readers, and churches differ on how to read such stories today.

Carry it today: Ask someone to go with you into something hard, or go with someone else.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Be not ashamed to be helped; for it is thy business to do thy duty like a soldier in the assault on a town. How then, if being lame thou canst not mount up on the battlements alone, but with the help of another it is possible?" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 7.7, Long translation.) The emperor admits that sometimes he can't climb the wall alone. Barak couldn't either. The Stoic and the Bible agree: needing help isn't failure. What matters is that the duty gets done.

The time. The events are set in the 12th century BC. Romans was written around AD 57.

What scholars broadly agree on. Judges 4 (prose) and Judges 5 (poetry) tell the same battle differently. Mount Tabor and the Kishon River are real places in the Jezreel Valley.

What it meant then. The story showed that God, not chariots, decides battles, and that God can use anyone.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw Jael's tent peg as the cross, and Jael as the church of the Gentiles.

What's debated or unknown. How the prose account in Judges 4 relates to the older poem in Judges 5, which differ in details, is much discussed.
