November 24th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Zechariah is one of the strangest prophets. Here God tells him to act out a parable: become shepherd of a flock "doomed to slaughter." He takes two staffs, names them Favor and Unity, and tends the sheep. But the flock and its owners despise him. When he asks for his wages, they pay him thirty shekels of silver, the price of a slave. God says, sarcastically, throw it into the treasury, "this lordly price at which I was valued by them." The prophet breaks both staffs, and a worthless shepherd takes over. Revelation answers the gloom: "Hallelujah! For the Lord God the Almighty reigns," and invites everyone to the wedding supper of the Lamb.

What it means: The good shepherd gets priced like a slave. People often undervalue those who care for them most. Christians hear Jesus here: sold for thirty pieces of silver, yet in the end it's his wedding feast.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: A good person, having done a good act, "does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act, as a vine goes on to produce again the grapes in season." (Meditations 5.6, Long.) The shepherd works without fair wages.
  • Sikhism: Today Sikhs celebrate Guru Nanak, who spent his father's money feeding the hungry and called it the true bargain.
  • Buddhism: On this full-moon uposatha, laypeople give alms to monks, generosity without asking a price.
  • Hinduism: On Dev Diwali, lamps line the ghats of Varanasi, offered freely to the gods.

Carry it today: Whose worth have you underpriced?

The theme

  • Favor and Unity: Two staffs.
  • Thirty shekels: The price of a slave.
  • Throw it into the treasury: God's sarcasm.
  • The marriage supper: The last word.

Yesterday, Moses asked God for a shepherd for his people. Today, the shepherd comes and the people pay him off.

1. A Flock Doomed to Slaughter

"So on behalf of the sheep merchants, I became the shepherd of the flock doomed to slaughter."

  • Bad owners: The merchants buy and sell the sheep and feel no guilt.
  • Acting it out: Like other prophets, Zechariah performs his message.

2. Favor and Unity

"I took two staffs; one I named Favor, the other I named Unity, and I tended the sheep."

  • What a shepherd gives: God's grace, and a people held together.
  • Broken: When he's rejected, he breaks Favor, then Unity, the bond between Judah and Israel.

3. Thirty Shekels of Silver

"I then said to them, 'If it seems right to you, give me my wages, but if not, keep them.' So they weighed out as my wages thirty shekels of silver."

  • A slave's price: Exodus 21:32 sets thirty shekels as compensation for a slave gored by an ox.
  • Judas: Matthew says the chief priests paid Judas thirty pieces of silver, and later quotes this passage (Matthew 26:15; 27:9–10).

4. This Lordly Price

"Then the Lord said to me, 'Throw it into the treasury'—this lordly price at which I was valued by them." "So I took the thirty shekels of silver and threw them into the treasury in the house of the Lord."

  • Sarcasm: "Lordly price" is ironic: an insult to God himself.
  • Thrown back: Judas, too, threw the silver into the Temple (Matthew 27:5).

5. The Marriage Supper

Zechariah ends with a curse on the "worthless shepherd, who deserts the flock." But Revelation gets the last word: "Hallelujah! For the Lord God the Almighty reigns." "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb." The shepherd sold for thirty silver pieces is the Lamb at the wedding feast. Undervalued love wins in the end.

For Christians today

The thirty pieces of silver became one of the most famous prices in history.

  • The oldest readings: From Matthew onward, Christians read Zechariah 11 as a prophecy of Jesus betrayed and sold.
  • Catholic: Today's martyrs, Andrew Dung-Lac and companions, were Vietnamese priests and laypeople who paid with their lives rather than deny Christ.
  • Orthodox and Episcopal: Catherine of Alexandria, honored today, is the legendary young scholar who debated the emperor's philosophers.
  • Protestant and evangelical: "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb" is often read at communion.
  • Thanksgiving week: A good day to thank the underpaid people who care for us: teachers, nurses, aides, caregivers.

Carry it today: Thank someone whose care you've taken for granted.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"So a man when he has done a good act, does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act, as a vine goes on to produce again the grapes in season." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.6, Long translation.) The shepherd in Zechariah was paid an insult. Marcus would say: a vine doesn't wait for applause before producing grapes. Do the good, and go on to the next one.

  • Purnima: "full moon."
  • Krittika: "the cutters," the Pleiades.
  • Dev Diwali: "Diwali of the gods."
  • Dana: "giving."
Sikh TraditionWiki ↗

Today's readings through a Sikh lens: Guru Nanak's birthday.

  • Gurpurab: Today Sikhs celebrate the birth of Guru Nanak (1469), founder of the faith, with an unbroken reading of the Guru Granth Sahib, processions and langar for all.
  • The true bargain: Given twenty rupees by his father to strike a good deal, young Nanak fed hungry holy men, calling it Sacha Sauda, "the true bargain." His father thought he'd been cheated; he'd found the real value.

Hukamnama (the verse drawn each morning from the Guru Granth Sahib at the Golden Temple and read by Sikhs worldwide): Ang 704, Raag Jaitsri, by Guru Arjan. SikhNet's English opens: "God is lofty, unapproachable and infinite." A fitting verse for Guru Nanak's birthday.

The time. Zechariah 9–14, "Second Zechariah," was likely written in the late Persian or early Greek period (5th–4th century BC), later than chapters 1–8 (520–518 BC). Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. Zechariah 11 is one of the hardest passages in the prophets; no one is sure which leaders the shepherds represent.

What it meant then. It reflected bitter disappointment with Judah's rulers and a split between communities ("Judah and Israel").

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the rejected shepherd as Christ, Favor as the covenant with the Gentiles, and Unity as the bond with Israel.

What's debated or unknown. Whether to read "treasury" or "potter" in verse 13 (the Hebrew says "potter"; Matthew picks that up with the potter's field).
