November 23rd, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Moses has led Israel for forty years. Now God tells him to climb a mountain and look at the promised land, because he will die before entering it. Moses doesn't argue. His first thought is for the people: Lord, appoint someone to lead them, so they won't be like sheep without a shepherd. God answers: "Take Joshua son of Nun, a man in whom is the spirit, and lay your hand upon him." In front of the priest and the whole community, Moses lays his hands on Joshua and gives him some of his authority. In 2 Timothy, Paul, chained in prison, does the same for Timothy: "If we endure, we will also reign with him."

What it means: Great leaders plan for the day they're gone. Moses could have grieved his own loss; instead he thought of the flock. Passing the torch isn't failure. It's the last act of faithful leadership.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Everything has two handles, the one by which it may be carried, the other by which it cannot." (Enchiridion 43.) Moses picked up his ending by the right handle.
  • Judaism: The rabbis said Moses' face was like the sun and Joshua's like the moon, reflecting the light he received (Bava Batra 75a, paraphrased).
  • Sikhism: Guru Nanak placed his hand on Bhai Lehna, naming him Guru Angad; tomorrow Sikhs celebrate Guru Nanak's birthday.
  • Islam: Before he died, the Prophet asked Abu Bakr to lead the prayers in his place (Bukhari, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Who will carry on what you started?

The theme

  • Sheep without a shepherd: His first worry.
  • Lay your hand on him: Passing it on.
  • He remains faithful: Paul's hymn.

Yesterday, the church crowned Christ the King. Today, an old shepherd hands his flock to a new one.

1. Moses on the Mountain

God tells Moses to climb the mountain and see the land he has given Israel, because Moses, like Aaron, will die without entering it (Numbers 27:12–14, paraphrased).

  • No argument: Moses doesn't plead for himself.
  • The view: He sees the promise, even if he won't hold it.

2. Sheep Without a Shepherd

Moses asks God, the God of the spirits of all flesh, to appoint a leader who will go out and come in before the people, so they won't be like sheep without a shepherd (Numbers 27:16–17, paraphrased).

  • Jesus echoes it: Seeing the crowds, Jesus had compassion on them because they were like sheep without a shepherd (Matthew 9:36, paraphrased).
  • Same name: "Joshua" in Hebrew is the same name as "Jesus."

3. Lay Your Hand Upon Him

"So the Lord said to Moses, 'Take Joshua son of Nun, a man in whom is the spirit, and lay your hand upon him.'"

  • Publicly: Moses does it in front of the priest Eleazar and the whole congregation.
  • Origin of ordination: Jewish semicha and Christian ordination both trace back to this laying on of hands.

4. Some of Your Authority

"You shall give him some of your authority."

  • Some, not all: Joshua will need the priest's counsel too.
  • Shared leadership: No one carries it alone.

5. He Remains Faithful

Paul, in prison, writes to Timothy: "The saying is sure: If we have died with him, we will also live with him; if we endure, we will also reign with him." "If we are faithless, he remains faithful—he cannot deny himself." Leaders come and go; Moses dies, Paul is chained, Joshua and Timothy take up the work. The faithfulness that holds the flock together belongs to God.

For Christians today

Passing on leadership is a living question in every church.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers saw Joshua, who led Israel across the Jordan, as a figure of Jesus, who leads us into the promised land.
  • Catholic and Orthodox: Bishops are ordained by the laying on of hands in a line they trace to the apostles; today's saint, Clement, was one of the earliest bishops of Rome.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many churches lay hands on new pastors, missionaries and elders, and pray over people being sent out.
  • Catholic: Today's Gospel praises the poor widow who gave her last two coins, all she had.
  • Live debates today: Churches wrestle with succession: aging pastors, declining clergy, and who gets hands laid on them, including women.

Carry it today: Teach someone one thing you know.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Everything has two handles, the one by which it may be carried, the other by which it cannot." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 43, Higginson translation.) Moses could have picked up his fate by the handle of bitterness: forty years, and I don't get in. Instead he picked it up by the other handle: what do my people need? Epictetus says every hard thing offers that choice.

What scholars broadly agree on. Joshua

The time. Numbers is set at the end of Israel's forty years in the wilderness, traditionally the 13th century BC; it reached its final form much later. 2 Timothy claims to be Paul's last letter, from prison in Rome in the mid-60s AD; many scholars think a follower wrote it later.

What scholars broadly agree on. Joshua was remembered as Moses' assistant from the beginning (Exodus 17, 24, 33), and the book of Joshua picks up where Deuteronomy ends.

What it meant then. For Israel, the story showed that God's covenant didn't depend on one man; it continued through new leaders.

The allegorical readings. The fathers noted that the Law (Moses) couldn't bring the people into the land; only Joshua, whose name means "the Lord saves," could.

What's debated or unknown. Whether Paul wrote 2 Timothy, and how historical the conquest under Joshua was.
