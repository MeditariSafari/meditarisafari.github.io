November 2nd, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Israel has crossed the Jordan. Before the river closes again, Joshua sends twelve men back into the riverbed, one from each tribe, to carry out a stone each from the very spot where the priests stood. They pile them up where they camp. "When your children ask in time to come, 'What do these stones mean?'" Joshua says, tell them: Israel crossed here on dry ground. The psalm pictures children "like olive shoots around your table," and Paul calls the believers he has left behind his "glory and joy." Today, on All Souls' Day, the church remembers everyone who has died in faith.

What it means: Memory is a kind of faithfulness. The stones weren't for the people who crossed; they were for the children who would ask. All Souls' Day and the Day of the Dead do the same work: they set up stones, light candles and tell the stories, so the people who carried us aren't forgotten.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Pass then through this little space of time conformably to nature, and end thy journey in content, just as an olive falls off when it is ripe, blessing nature who produced it, and thanking the tree on which it grew." (Meditations 4.48, Long.)
  • Judaism: Jews say Kaddish for the dead and light a yahrzeit candle on the anniversary of a death. A traditional blessing for the departed: may their memory be a blessing.
  • Islam: The Prophet taught that when a person dies, their deeds end except three: ongoing charity, useful knowledge, and a righteous child who prays for them (Muslim, paraphrased).
  • Confucius: He taught that when the rites for the dead are kept with care and the departed are remembered, the people's virtue grows full (Analects 1.9, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Who carried you across, and what stone will you set up to remember them?

Yesterday Israel crossed the Jordan on All Saints' Day. Today they mark the place, on All Souls' Day, when the church remembers the ones who went before.

1. Stones from the Riverbed

"Take twelve stones from here out of the middle of the Jordan, from the place where the priests' feet stood, carry them over with you."

  • One per tribe: Every tribe carries a piece of the story. No one's memory is left out.
  • From the hardest place: The stones come from where the priests stood waiting in the riverbed.

2. What Do These Stones Mean?

"When your children ask in time to come, 'What do those stones mean to you?' then you shall tell them that the waters of the Jordan were cut off in front of the ark."

  • Made to be asked about: The memorial works by provoking questions. Faith is handed down in answers to children.
  • "To you": The question is personal. What does it mean to you, not just to history?

3. Twelve More in the River

"Joshua set up twelve stones in the middle of the Jordan, in the place where the feet of the priests bearing the ark of the covenant had stood, and they are there to this day."

  • Hidden memorial: One pile stands at Gilgal for all to see; the other lies under the water, known only to God.
  • All Souls: Many faithful lives are like those river stones: unseen, but there.

4. The Waters Return

When "the soles of the priests' feet touched dry ground, the waters of the Jordan returned to their place and overflowed all its banks, as before."

  • A window of grace: The river stays open until the last person crosses, and then closes.
  • Why remember: Because the moment passes, it needs remembering.

5. So That All Peoples May Know

"For the Lord your God dried up the waters of the Jordan for you until you crossed over, as the Lord your God did to the Red Sea." "So that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the Lord is mighty." The stones aren't nostalgia. They are testimony for the children, and for the world. Paul, separated from the Thessalonians, longs "to see you face to face" and calls them "our glory and joy." On All Souls' Day, the church does both things Joshua did: it remembers the ones who crossed before us, and it tells the story so the next generation will know the river can be crossed.

For Christians today

All Souls' Day and the stones at Gilgal both speak to how Christians remember their dead.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christians compared the twelve stones to the twelve apostles, carried out of the waters of baptism to become the foundation of the church (Ephesians 2:20; Revelation 21:14).
  • Catholic: All Souls' Day began with Abbot Odilo of Cluny around 998, who set it the day after All Saints. Catholics pray for the faithful departed, visit graves, and priests may celebrate three Masses today.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Reformers objected to prayers for the dead, and many Protestant churches fold All Souls into All Saints' Sunday. Many still keep the day as a time to give thanks for loved ones who have died.
  • Latin America: The Day of the Dead blends Catholic All Souls with older Indigenous customs. Families clean graves, build altars and share meals with the memory of the dead.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox remember the dead on several "Soul Saturdays" through the year rather than on November 2.

Carry it today: Tell someone the story of a person who handed you faith.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Pass then through this little space of time conformably to nature, and end thy journey in content, just as an olive falls off when it is ripe, blessing nature who produced it, and thanking the tree on which it grew." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.48, Long translation.) On All Souls' Day, Marcus offers a gentle picture of death: a ripe olive dropping from its tree, grateful. Psalm 128 pictures children "like olive shoots around your table." The same tree that lets one olive fall sends up new shoots.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: the undying soul.

  • The Hindu lens: The Gita teaches that the soul (atman) is never born and never dies, and that death is like putting off worn-out clothes for new (2:20, 2:22, paraphrased). Hindus honor ancestors in shraddha rites.
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that every soul (jiva) is eternal; a peaceful, mindful death is valued, and remembering the tirthankaras, who crossed over, is central. Tirthankara means "ford-maker," one who makes a crossing.

Today is Krishna Ashtami (ending at 1:10 PM India time), then Navami, with the moon moving from Pushya into Ashlesha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Tirthankara: "ford-maker," one who makes a crossing.
  • Atman: "self, soul."
  • Navami: "the ninth."
  • Ashlesha: "the embrace."

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; 1 Thessalonians was written around AD 50. All Souls' Day dates from about AD 998 at Cluny in France.

What scholars broadly agree on. Joshua 4 seems to combine two traditions: the stones at Gilgal and the stones in the river. Gilgal was an important early shrine, and the story likely explained a stone circle known there.

What it meant then. For later Israel, the stones at Gilgal tied their worship to the moment they entered the land. For Paul, the Thessalonians were living proof that his work had borne fruit.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the twelve stones as the twelve apostles and the crossing as baptism.

What's debated or unknown. The exact site of Gilgal near Jericho is not certain, and archaeologists have proposed several locations.
