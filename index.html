“After Joshua's generation dies, a new generation grows up that doesn't know the Lord, and Israel turns to other gods.”
November 12th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: On Monday, Joshua died, and Israel stayed faithful as long as the elders who had seen God's work were alive. Today the book of Judges picks up the story. "That whole generation was gathered to their ancestors, and another generation grew up after them who did not know the Lord or the work that he had done for Israel." They hadn't seen the Red Sea or the Jordan part. They knew the Canaanite gods next door better than the God of their parents. So they turned to the Baals, and "they were in great distress."

What it means: Faith isn't inherited automatically. Each generation has to come to know God for itself, and someone has to tell the story. Monday asked what will witness to our promises after we're gone. Today's answer is sobering: if no one passes it on, a generation can grow up that simply doesn't know.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen." (Meditations 6.30, Long.)
  • Judaism: The Passover Haggadah is built on the command to tell your children (Exodus 13:8), so each generation knows.
  • Confucius: He taught that the root of goodness is passed through family and teaching (Analects 1.2, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Qur'an warns against following the ways of the forefathers blindly, without knowledge (2:170, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Who will know the Lord because of you?

This week the lectionary has moved from Joshua's death (Monday) to Ezra's reading (Tuesday) and Jeremiah's new covenant (Wednesday). Today it steps back to the dark years after Joshua.

1. The Elders Who Had Seen

"The people served the Lord all the days of Joshua and all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua, who had seen all the great work that the Lord had done for Israel."

  • Eyewitnesses: Their faith rested on what they had seen.
  • Repeated: These words echo Joshua 24:31, Monday's reading, almost exactly.

2. Another Generation

"Moreover, that whole generation was gathered to their ancestors, and another generation grew up after them who did not know the Lord or the work that he had done for Israel."

  • "Did not know": In Hebrew, to know God is to have a relationship, not just facts.
  • The gap: Somewhere the story stopped being told.

3. The Baals

"Then the Israelites did what was evil in the sight of the Lord and served the Baals."

  • Baal: The Canaanite storm god, believed to bring rain and good harvests.
  • The easy choice: In a farming land, the local god of rain looked more useful than the God of the desert.

4. They Abandoned the Lord

"They abandoned the Lord, the God of their ancestors, who had brought them out of the land of Egypt."

  • The God of their ancestors: Known to their parents, but not to them.
  • Brought out of Egypt: The founding story, forgotten.

5. Eyes Lifted Up

The psalm offers the way back: "To you I lift up my eyes." Judges goes on to tell how, again and again, Israel cried out in distress, and God raised up judges to rescue them. The cycle of forgetting isn't the end. But the book's lesson is plain: faith must be handed on on purpose, story by story, generation by generation. Wednesday's promise of a law written on the heart is God's answer to exactly this problem.

For Christians today

Judges 2 speaks to every church that worries about the next generation.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read Judges as a mirror of the soul's cycle of sin, repentance and grace.
  • Catholic: Today's saint, Josaphat Kuntsevych, a Ukrainian archbishop, was killed in 1623 for working toward unity between Eastern and Roman Christians. Rome later canonized him as a martyr for unity.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Charles Simeon, commemorated today, preached at Holy Trinity, Cambridge, for 54 years and trained generations of clergy, a man who made sure the next generation knew.
  • Psalm 78: This week's psalm was written for exactly this: so that the next generation, and children not yet born, would know God's works (Psalm 78:6, paraphrased).
  • Live debates today: How to pass on faith when many young people leave church is one of the most discussed questions in Christianity today.

Carry it today: Tell a story of God's faithfulness to someone younger.

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.30, Long translation.) Marcus, the most powerful man in the world, warns himself not to be soaked through by the culture of the palace. Israel was dyed by the culture of Canaan. Both ask: what color is your soul taking on from what surrounds you?

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: a teaching handed down.

  • The Hindu lens: The parampara, the unbroken chain from teacher to student, is how Hindu wisdom is passed on. The Gita warns that over time the teaching can be lost (4:2, paraphrased).
  • The Jain lens: Jains honor the 24 tirthankaras, teachers who appeared in each age to restore a path that had been forgotten.

Today is Shukla Tritiya (ending at 6:09 PM India time), then Shukla Chaturthi, in the month of Kartika, with the moon moving from Jyeshtha into Mula (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). The panchang lists no festival today.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Parampara: "one after another," a lineage.
  • Tritiya: "the third day."
  • Chaturthi: "the fourth day."
  • Mula: "the root."

The time. Judges is set in the 12th and 11th centuries BC, between the settlement in Canaan and the first kings. Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. Judges was shaped by later editors into a repeating cycle: sin, oppression, crying out, a rescuer, peace, and sin again. Archaeology shows Baal worship was widespread in Canaan.

What it meant then. For later readers, perhaps in exile, Judges explained disaster: Israel suffered when it forgot its God.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the judges as figures of Christ, the final deliverer.

What's debated or unknown. How much of the conquest and settlement happened as Joshua and Judges describe is debated; Judges itself shows a slower, messier process than Joshua.
