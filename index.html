The five-minute version

The readings: Jesus stops at the home of two sisters. Martha does what any good host would: she cooks, serves and runs the house. Mary sits at Jesus's feet and listens. Martha, fed up, asks Jesus to tell Mary to help. He answers gently: "Martha, Martha, you are anxious and worried about many things," but only one thing is needed, and Mary has chosen the better part. In Galatians, Paul says that when God called him, he didn't rush off to start a ministry. He went away to Arabia first.

What it means: Yesterday's story was about a man who stopped to help. Today's is about a woman who stopped to listen. Luke puts them side by side on purpose: love that acts and love that listens belong together. Jesus isn't scolding Martha for working. He's naming her worry and her resentment, which is what service turns into when it never stops to be fed. Mary's sitting isn't laziness. It's where real service comes from.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Judaism: the rabbis asked whether study or action is greater, and answered that study is, because it leads to action.
  • Islam: "in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest" (Qur'an 13:28).
  • Sikhism: remembrance (simran) and service (sewa) go together, like Mary and Martha in one person.
  • Stoicism: Marcus Aurelius said to cut away the unnecessary acts, and the unnecessary thoughts too.
  • Tao Te Ching: the one who follows the Tao does less each day, until nothing is left undone.

Carry it today: What are you "anxious and worried" about that keeps you from sitting still for five minutes?

The theme

  • Busy isn't the same as faithful: Martha isn't wrong to serve. She's wrong to think her worth is in the serving. When doing good becomes how we prove ourselves, it slowly turns into anxiety and resentment, and we start keeping score of who isn't helping.
  • Being comes before doing: Psalm 139 says God knew you before you were born, and Paul says God set him apart from his mother's womb. Before you accomplish anything, you're already known and loved. Mary's sitting is just believing that.
  • The desert comes first: Paul didn't go straight from his conversion into ministry. He disappeared into Arabia. Every real calling seems to need a season of stillness before it needs a schedule.

Yesterday Luke told us about a Samaritan who stopped for a stranger in a ditch. Today, right after, he tells us about a woman who stopped everything to listen. The two stories sit next to each other on purpose. Together they answer the lawyer's question about how to inherit eternal life: love your neighbor (the Samaritan) and love God (Mary). Most of us are better at one than the other.

1. Martha Isn't the Villain

Martha is doing exactly what her culture, and ours, says a good person does. A guest arrives, and she makes sure he's fed. Hospitality was a sacred duty in the ancient world; Abraham ran to serve his three visitors at Mamre.

  • The Real Problem: Luke says Martha was "distracted" by much serving. The Greek word, perispaō, means "pulled around" or "dragged in all directions." Her trouble isn't the work. It's that the work is pulling her apart.
  • The Tell: Her complaint gives her away: "Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me by myself to do the serving?" When service turns into "nobody appreciates me" and "why isn't she doing her part," it's no longer coming from love. It's coming from fear of not being enough.

2. Mary Sits Where Only Men Sat

"Sitting at someone's feet" was how a student sat with a teacher. Paul describes his own education the same way, raised "at the feet of Gamaliel" (Acts 22:3).

  • A Radical Seat: For a woman to take the disciple's place in a teacher's house would have shocked many people. Martha may be upset about more than dishes. Mary has stepped out of her assigned role.
  • Jesus Defends Her: He won't send her back to the kitchen. "It will not be taken from her." The better part is open to everyone, whatever role the world has handed them.

3. "Martha, Martha"

Jesus says her name twice, the way you speak to someone you love who's spinning out. It's the tenderest rebuke in the Gospels.

  • Many Things vs. One Thing: She's worried about "many things." Only one thing is needed. The anxious mind multiplies tasks; the centered heart keeps coming back to the one thing that makes the tasks worth doing.
  • Not a Ranking of People: Jesus isn't saying contemplatives are better than workers. He's saying that work cut off from presence starts to eat the worker.

4. Paul's Arabia

In Galatians, Paul describes a man on fire with religious zeal, so sure of himself that he persecuted the church. Then God, "who from my mother's womb had set me apart," revealed Christ to him.

  • He Didn't Rush: Paul says he did not consult "flesh and blood" and didn't run to Jerusalem to get credentials. He went to Arabia. We don't know what he did there, but it was a long, quiet stretch before he started preaching.
  • Zeal Without Stillness: Before his conversion, Paul was the ultimate Martha: busy, driven, sure he was serving God, and doing harm. His own story is the warning: activity without listening can turn violent.

5. The Better Part

Put the three readings together and a single picture comes into focus. Psalm 139 says you were known before you did anything. Paul learns he was chosen before he was born, by grace, not achievement. And Mary simply sits in that love and lets it fill her.

The Samaritan yesterday and Mary today aren't opposites. The Samaritan could stop for a stranger because something in him was already still enough to notice. Mary's listening is where that kind of compassion comes from. Martha's way, when it runs on anxiety, ends in burnout and resentment. Mary's way doesn't end in idleness. It ends in a love that can finally serve without keeping score.

For Christians today

The story of Martha and Mary is short, but Christians have argued about it for two thousand years.

  • The oldest reading: two kinds of life. Origen, Augustine and Gregory the Great read Martha as the active life and Mary as the contemplative life. Augustine preached that Martha's work, good as it is, belongs to this passing world, while Mary's part, resting in God, is what lasts forever. Medieval monks saw themselves in Mary.
  • Catholic: The Church has always held the two together. Saint Benedict's rule is summed up as ora et labora, "pray and work." Today's saint, Bruno, founded the Carthusians, who live mostly in silence and solitude, as Mary's part lived to the full. The Catechism calls prayer a living relationship with God, not a duty to tick off.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther read Mary as faith listening to God's Word, and Martha as the urge to earn God's favor by works. For many evangelicals today the story is a call to the daily "quiet time" with Scripture before the busyness starts.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church reads this Gospel on feasts of the Mother of God, joined to the verse "blessed are those who hear the word of God and keep it." Mary of Bethany becomes a picture of the Virgin Mary, who "kept all these things, pondering them in her heart."
  • A surprising twist: The medieval mystic Meister Eckhart flipped the story. He preached that Martha was the more mature sister, because she had learned to stay centered in God while working, and she was worried Mary would get stuck in pleasant feelings. Many Christians today find that reading freeing: the goal is a Martha with Mary's heart.
  • Live debates today: Christians argue about burnout in ministry and church cultures that measure faith by how busy you are. Others point out that this story has been used to keep women in the kitchen, when Jesus actually defends a woman's right to sit as a disciple.

Carry it today: Before you do one more thing for God today, can you spend a few minutes just being with God?

What is Stoicism? A school founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, who taught under a painted porch (stoa) that gave the school its name. Its best-known voices are Romans: Epictetus, a freed slave; Seneca, a statesman; and Marcus Aurelius, an emperor. The heart of it is knowing what's up to us (our judgments, choices and actions) and what isn't (other people, events, reputation), and spending our energy only on the first. Stoics aimed to live by four virtues, wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Occupy thyself with few things, says the philosopher, if thou wouldst be tranquil... For the greatest part of what we say and do being unnecessary, if a man takes this away, he will have more leisure and less uneasiness. Accordingly on every occasion a man should ask himself, Is this one of the unnecessary things? Now a man should take away not only unnecessary acts, but also, unnecessary thoughts." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.24, Long translation) Marcus is talking to Martha. She's anxious about "many things," and Jesus tells her only one is needed. Ask of each task today: is this one of the unnecessary things?

The time. Both readings come from the first generation of Christianity. Jesus's visit to Martha and Mary would have taken place around AD 30, near the end of his ministry, in Roman-ruled Judea. Luke wrote his Gospel about fifty years later, around AD 80–90, for mostly non-Jewish readers. Paul's conversion is usually dated to about AD 33–36, and he wrote Galatians around AD 50–55, so his letter is earlier than any of the Gospels. The "Arabia" he went to was almost certainly the Nabataean kingdom east and south of Damascus, whose capital was Petra. Paul says King Aretas's governor later tried to arrest him in Damascus (2 Corinthians 11:32–33), and since Aretas IV died around AD 40, that helps historians date these years.

What scholars broadly agree on. Almost all historians, religious or not, accept that Paul was a real person, a Pharisee who once persecuted Jesus's followers and then became the movement's most important missionary, and that Galatians is genuinely his letter. They also agree that the Gospels preserve memories of real people around Jesus, and Martha and Mary are named in two independent sources, Luke and John. John places the sisters in Bethany, a village about two miles from Jerusalem, with their brother Lazarus. Scholars agree, too, that sitting "at someone's feet" was the posture of a student with a teacher (Paul says he trained "at the feet of Gamaliel," Acts 22:3), and that women taking that place was unusual. Luke goes out of his way to show women as hosts, funders and disciples (Luke 8:1–3).

What it meant then. Most scholars read the story, in Luke's setting, as a lesson about discipleship: listening to Jesus comes first, and service flows from it. Luke places it right after the Good Samaritan on purpose. Together the two stories act out the two great commandments the lawyer just quoted, love of neighbor and love of God. Many also see it affirming that women can be full disciples. For Paul, Galatians 1 is a defense of his authority: his message came from God, not from the Jerusalem leaders, which is why he stresses how briefly he met Peter and that he went off to Arabia first.

The allegorical readings. From the early church on, Christians read the sisters as symbols. Origen, Augustine and Gregory the Great saw Martha as the active life and Mary as the contemplative life, and that reading shaped monasticism for a thousand years. Some medieval writers, most famously Meister Eckhart, turned it around and praised Martha as the more mature sister, who had learned to stay with God in the middle of work. These readings are honored traditions, but scholars note they're interpretations layered on later, not what Luke himself says.

What's debated or unknown. The oldest manuscripts of Luke don't agree on Jesus's exact words: most read "one thing is needed," some read "few things are needed, or only one," and at least one leaves it out. Luke doesn't name the village, and his timeline (he tells the story far from Jerusalem) doesn't match John's, so we don't know exactly when the visit happened. Acts 9 tells Paul's early years differently from Galatians, without Arabia, and nobody knows what Paul did there or how long he stayed.

  • Joseph Campbell: In The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), the mythologist traced one pattern through the world's myths: the hero hears a call, leaves the familiar world, goes through trials in a strange place (often a desert, forest or underworld), and comes back changed, with a gift for his people. He argued these are archetypes shared across human history, the same story told in every culture: Moses in the wilderness of Midian, the Buddha under the Bodhi tree, Jesus's forty days in the desert, Muhammad in the cave of Hira. (Paraphrased.) Paul's story fits it exactly: the call on the Damascus road, the departure into Arabia, the return to Jerusalem with a message. Mary's chair is the quieter version of the same journey, a step out of the busy world to be changed before going back into it.
  • Eckhart Tolle: He teaches that most anxiety comes from living in the mind's future, the next task and the next problem, instead of the present moment, the only place life actually happens. (Our summary of his ideas, not his words.) Martha is three steps ahead in the meal. Mary is in the room with Jesus, now.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: He taught that you should wash the dishes in order to wash the dishes, not just to get them done, so that the work itself becomes meditation. (Paraphrased from The Miracle of Mindfulness.) He would have told Martha that the kitchen can be Mary's chair too.
  • Gurdjieff: His central practice was "self-remembering": staying aware of yourself while you act, instead of being swallowed by the task. He taught that most people live mechanically, like machines reacting to whatever pulls them. (Paraphrased.) Martha was being pulled around. Mary remembered herself, and remembered who was in the room.
  • Tao Te Ching: "He who devotes himself to learning (seeks) from day to day to increase (his knowledge); he who devotes himself to the Tao (seeks) from day to day to diminish (his doing)... Having arrived at this point of non-action, there is nothing which he does not do." (Ch. 48, Legge translation.) Martha keeps adding tasks. Mary has subtracted everything but the one thing, and from there, nothing is left undone.
  • Martin Luther King Jr.: Early in the Montgomery bus boycott, late one night after a threatening phone call, King sat at his kitchen table, exhausted and afraid, ready to quit. He prayed out loud, and later said he heard an inner voice telling him to stand up for justice and promising he would never be alone. (Paraphrased from his account in Stride Toward Freedom.) His greatest work came out of that moment of stopping, not out of more striving. Mary's chair before the Samaritan's road.
  • Confucius: "Learning without thought is labor lost; thought without learning is perilous." (Analects 2.15, Legge translation.) Martha is all doing, no reflection. The point isn't to choose one. It's to hold both.
  • Nelson Mandela: In letters from prison, Mandela wrote that the cell gave him the chance to look honestly at his own conduct, to work on his weaknesses and build on what was good in him, and that time alone helped make him who he became. (Paraphrased from a 1975 letter to Winnie Mandela.) Twenty-seven years was his Arabia. The man who came out could lead a nation without revenge.
  • Marcus Aurelius: "Men seek retreats for themselves, houses in the country, sea-shores, and mountains... But this is altogether a mark of the most common sort of men, for it is in thy power whenever thou shalt choose to retire into thyself. For nowhere either with more quiet or more freedom from trouble does a man retire than into his own soul." (Meditations 4.3, Long translation.) Mary didn't need a retreat center. She found the quiet place right there in Martha's busy house.
  • Ram Dass: His most famous book is simply titled Be Here Now. (Title only.) Three words that sum up what Jesus told Martha.
  • Buddha: "Even though a speech be a thousand (of words), but made up of senseless words, one word of sense is better, which if a man hears, he becomes quiet." (Dhammapada 100, Müller translation.) Mary heard the one word that made her quiet.
  • Bhagavad Gita: "Steadfast a lamp burns sheltered from the wind; / Such is the likeness of the Yogi's mind / Shut from sense-storms and burning bright to Heaven." (Ch. 6, Arnold translation, The Song Celestial.) Martha is the flame in the wind. Mary is the sheltered lamp.
  • Gregory of Nyssa: In his Life of Moses, he described the spiritual life as an endless climb into God, where every step of knowing God opens a desire to know more, and he said this is the one thing that is never finished. (Paraphrased.) That's what Jesus means by the part that "will not be taken from her."
  • Rumi: The Sufi poet often wrote of a lover so absorbed in the Beloved that the world's errands fall away, and of the soul's need to be silent to hear. (Paraphrased.) Mary at Jesus's feet is Rumi's lover, forgetting everything but the face in front of her.
  • Desert Fathers: A brother asked Abba Moses for a word, and the old man told him to go and sit in his cell, and his cell would teach him everything. (Sayings of the Desert Fathers, paraphrased.) Paul's Arabia, Bruno's hermitage and Mary's place on the floor are all that cell.
  • Kahlil Gibran: "You talk when you cease to be at peace with your thoughts; And when you can no longer dwell in the solitude of your heart you live in your lips, and sound is a diversion and a pastime." (The Prophet, "On Talking.") Martha's complaint is what comes out when we can't stand our own silence.
  • Viktor Frankl: He wrote about the "Sunday neurosis," the emptiness people feel when the busy week stops and they have to face the lack of meaning underneath. (Paraphrased from Man's Search for Meaning.) Staying busy can be a way of never sitting with Mary's question: what is the one thing?
  • Alan Watts: He compared life to music: the point of a song isn't to reach the end, and the point of dancing isn't to get somewhere on the floor. (Paraphrased.) Martha is racing to finish the meal. Mary knows the visit itself is the point.
  • Slow conversion: Francis took a lifetime to learn to see God in every creature, and Paul needed his years in Arabia (CAC).
  • Made whole by listening: God's people are healed by hearing the word together, Mary's chair shared by a whole community (SSJE).

7AA Daily Reflections: "Facing Ourselves"(Twelve Steps and Twelve Traditions, p. 49)Fear says don't look inside, but the look turns out smaller than the fear.

The reflection is about the moment of starting an honest self-inventory. Fear whispers that we don't dare look, and so we put it off. But once we finally sit down and face ourselves, the job turns out far smaller than our dread of it, and the illusions that were holding us back start to fall away. It's Mary's chair in recovery language: the courage to sit still and look.
