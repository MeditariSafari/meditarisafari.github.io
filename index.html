“On his deathbed, Jacob blesses his grandsons in the name of the God who shepherded him all his life, and deliberately puts the younger boy first.”
November 19th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jacob is old, nearly blind, and living in Egypt. Joseph brings his two sons for a blessing, placing the older one, Manasseh, at Jacob's right hand. But Jacob crosses his arms and lays his right hand on the younger, Ephraim. He blesses them in the name of "the God who has been my shepherd all my life to this day." Joseph tries to move his father's hand. "I know, my son, I know," Jacob says. Both boys will be great, but the younger will be greater. "So he put Ephraim ahead of Manasseh." Psalm 100 gives thanks: "We are his people and the sheep of his pasture."

What it means: Jacob had been a liar, a runaway and a grieving father. Looking back from the end, he doesn't see a self-made life. He sees a shepherd who led him every day. And he passes the blessing on, not by the usual rules of who comes first.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper." (Meditations 1.1, Long.) He opens his whole book thanking those who shaped him.
  • Judaism: To this day, parents bless their sons on Friday night: May God make you like Ephraim and Manasseh.
  • Islam: The Qur'an tells of Jacob's deathbed question to his sons: whom will you worship after me? (2:133, paraphrased).
  • Sikhism: Gratitude, remembering the Giver in every gift, is at the heart of Sikh prayer.

Carry it today: Who has shepherded you, and whom are you blessing?

Yesterday, Esther spoke up to save a people. Today, an old man blesses the next generation.

1. My Shepherd All My Life

"The God before whom my ancestors Abraham and Isaac walked, the God who has been my shepherd all my life to this day."

  • First shepherd: This is one of the earliest times the Bible calls God a shepherd, long before Psalm 23.
  • A shepherd himself: Jacob spent twenty years tending Laban's flocks; he knew what shepherding cost.

2. Bless the Boys

"The angel who has redeemed me from all harm, bless the boys, and in them let my name be perpetuated."

  • Adopted: Jacob makes Joseph's Egyptian-born sons his own, so they become two of the tribes of Israel.
  • The angel: Perhaps remembering the one he wrestled at the Jabbok.

3. Crossed Hands

Joseph puts Manasseh, the firstborn, at his father's right hand. Jacob crosses his arms.

  • A pattern: Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph over his brothers, now Ephraim over Manasseh. God keeps choosing the younger.
  • Remembering: Jacob himself stole his blind father's blessing. Now blind, he gives one freely.

4. I Know, My Son, I Know

"I know, my son, I know." "So he put Ephraim ahead of Manasseh."

  • Not a mistake: The old man isn't confused. He blesses on purpose.
  • Both blessed: Manasseh will become great too, Jacob says.

5. The Sheep of His Pasture

Psalm 100 answers: "Know that the Lord is God. It is he who made us, and we are his; we are his people and the sheep of his pasture." "For the Lord is good; his steadfast love endures forever and his faithfulness to all generations." One generation's shepherd becomes the next generation's blessing. Revelation shows where the flock ends up: with the Lamb on Mount Zion, singing a new song.

For Christians today

Christians read Jacob's blessing in many ways.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christians saw Jacob's crossed arms as the sign of the cross, and the younger son as the Gentile church receiving the greater blessing.
  • Hebrews: "By faith Jacob, when dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph" (Hebrews 11:21).
  • Catholic: Today's Gospel has Jesus weeping over Jerusalem, a shepherd grieving a flock that won't come.
  • Protestant and evangelical: "The Lord is my shepherd" piety grows from this verse; many families pray a blessing over children at bedtime or milestones.
  • Thanksgiving week: Psalm 100 is a classic Thanksgiving psalm, a week before the American holiday.

Carry it today: Speak a blessing to someone younger than you.

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper." "From the reputation and remembrance of my father, modesty and a manly character." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 1.1–2, Long translation.) The most powerful man in the world began his private notebook not with himself but with a list of thanks to everyone who formed him. Jacob, at the end, does the same: the God who shepherded me, my ancestors who walked before me.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: blessing the young.

  • The Hindu lens: Children touch the feet of elders and receive ashirvad, a blessing, with a hand placed on the head, much like Jacob's hands on the boys.
  • The Jain lens: Jains honor the teacher lineage through the Namokar Mantra, bowing to all who walked the path before.

Today is Shukla Dashami for most of the day, in the month of Kartika, with the moon moving from Shatabhisha into Purva Bhadrapada (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Tomorrow is Prabodhini Ekadashi, when Vishnu is said to wake from his four-month sleep.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Dashami: "the tenth day."
  • Ashirvad: "blessing."
  • Purva Bhadrapada: "the former lucky feet."
  • Prabodhini: "the awakening."

The time. The Joseph story is set in Egypt in the patriarchal age, traditionally the early second millennium BC; it was likely written down much later. Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. Ephraim and Manasseh became two of the largest tribes; Ephraim became so dominant that the northern kingdom itself was sometimes called "Ephraim" (as in Hosea).

What it meant then. The story explained why the younger tribe outranked the older, and gave Egyptian-born sons a full place in Israel.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the crossed hands as the cross, with the blessing passing from the "elder" people to the "younger" church; Jewish readers see in the boys the first brothers in Genesis who didn't fight.

What's debated or unknown. When the Joseph story was written, and how much it reflects real Egyptian history.
