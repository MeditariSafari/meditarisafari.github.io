“Joshua prays and the sun stands still. Jesus warns against honoring God with the lips while the heart is far away.”
November 4th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Five Amorite kings attack Gibeon, a city that made peace with Israel. Joshua marches all night to defend them, and in the middle of the battle he prays aloud before all Israel: "Sun, stand still at Gibeon." The text says the sun did not hurry to set for about a whole day, and that there was never a day like it, "when the Lord heeded a human voice." In Matthew, religious leaders from Jerusalem complain that Jesus' disciples don't wash their hands the traditional way. Jesus turns the question around: they keep their traditions but break God's commands. He quotes Isaiah: "This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me."

What it means: Joshua's prayer is bold and wholehearted: he asks for the impossible, in front of everyone, for the sake of people he had promised to protect. The Pharisees' religion is careful about outward forms but has drifted from the heart. God hears a human voice when the heart is in it. The question is whether our words to God come from close to the heart, or from far away.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "It is in thy power whenever thou shalt choose to retire into thyself. For nowhere either with more quiet or more freedom from trouble does a man retire than into his own soul." (Meditations 4.3, Long.)
  • Proverbs: "Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life." (4:23, NRSVUE, via Verse & Voice.)
  • Sikh: Guru Nanak taught that bathing at pilgrim places is worthless without a clean heart (Japji, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Prophet said God does not look at your appearance or wealth, but at your hearts and deeds (Muslim, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Is your heart as near to God as your words?

The theme

  • Sun, stand still: A bold prayer, prayed in front of everyone.

Over the past week Israel crossed the Jordan and took Jericho. Today Joshua defends the Gibeonites, and in Matthew, Jesus confronts empty religion.

1. Sun, Stand Still

Joshua "said in the sight of Israel, 'Sun, stand still at Gibeon, and Moon, in the valley of Aijalon.'"

  • In the sight of Israel: Joshua prays publicly, risking embarrassment. It's a prayer of total trust.
  • For others: He is fighting to protect the Gibeonites, who had tricked Israel into a treaty. Joshua keeps his word anyway.

2. The Lord Heeded a Human Voice

"There has been no day like it before or since, when the Lord heeded a human voice, for the Lord fought for Israel."

  • God listens: The wonder isn't only the sun; it's that God listened to a person.
  • The Book of Jashar: The poem is quoted from an older book of songs, now lost (also cited in 2 Samuel 1:18).

3. The Tradition of the Elders

"Why do your disciples break the tradition of the elders? For they do not wash their hands before they eat."

  • Ritual washing: This was about ritual purity, not hygiene. The practice was meant to bring holiness into daily life.
  • From Jerusalem: These critics came a long way to find fault.

4. For the Sake of Your Tradition

"And why do you break the commandment of God for the sake of your tradition?" "So, for the sake of your tradition, you nullify the word of God."

  • Korban: Jesus points to a practice of dedicating money to the Temple, which let someone avoid supporting aging parents.
  • Honor your parents: Tradition was used to dodge the plain command to care for father and mother.

5. Lips and Hearts

"This people honors me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me; in vain do they worship me, teaching human precepts as doctrines." Jesus quotes Isaiah 29:13, a word given seven centuries earlier. Joshua's prayer and the Pharisees' rules stand side by side today: one is a wholehearted cry for the sake of others; the other is careful outward religion that has lost its center. Tradition is a gift when it carries the heart toward God and neighbor. It becomes a wall when it replaces them. Proverbs says it simply: "Keep your heart with all vigilance, for from it flow the springs of life."

For Christians today

Both readings have been hotly discussed through history.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine taught that God values the heart's love above outward observance. The fathers read Joshua's prayer as a sign of the power of faithful prayer.
  • Galileo: Joshua 10 was famously cited against Galileo's claim that the earth moves around the sun. Today most Christians read the passage as poetry, not astronomy.
  • Catholic: Catholics value Tradition with a capital T, handed down from the apostles, but distinguish it from human customs that can change.
  • Protestant and evangelical: The Reformers used Matthew 15 to criticize human traditions they saw as burdening the gospel.
  • Live debates today: How to tell living tradition from empty custom is a question every church faces.

Carry it today: Pray one honest prayer today, from the heart rather than habit.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Men seek retreats for themselves, houses in the country, sea-shores, and mountains; and thou too art wont to desire such things very much. But this is altogether a mark of the most common sort of men, for it is in thy power whenever thou shalt choose to retire into thyself. For nowhere either with more quiet or more freedom from trouble does a man retire than into his own soul." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.3, Long translation.) The emperor's retreat is not a villa but his own soul. Jesus warns of worship that stays on the lips while the heart is far away. Marcus points to where the heart lives: within, always within reach.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: devotion of the heart.

  • The Hindu lens: Krishna says he accepts even a leaf, a flower, fruit or water, if offered with love (Gita 9:26, paraphrased). The heart, not the size of the offering, matters.
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that inner intention (bhava) matters more than outward action; the same act can bind or free depending on the heart behind it.

Today is Krishna Dashami (ending at 11:03 AM India time), then Ekadashi, with the moon moving from Magha into Purva Phalguni (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Tomorrow is Rama Ekadashi, a fasting day for Vishnu. (Ekadashi, the eleventh lunar day, is kept twice a month with fasting and prayer.)

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Bhava: "feeling, inner disposition."
  • Ekadashi: "the eleventh."
  • Purva Phalguni: "the former reddish one," a star in Leo.
  • Dashami: "the tenth."

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; Matthew wrote around AD 80–90.

What scholars broadly agree on. Joshua 10:12–13 quotes an ancient war poem from the Book of Jashar. Many scholars read it as poetic language, like Judges 5:20, where the stars fight from heaven. Handwashing before meals was a Pharisaic practice for ritual purity in Jesus' time.

What it meant then. For Israel, the poem celebrated God fighting for them. For Matthew's community, Jesus' words helped explain their break with some Pharisaic practices.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the sun standing still as the light of Christ that does not set on those who trust him.

What's debated or unknown. Whether the story recalls an eclipse or other unusual event, or is purely poetic, is debated.
