November 20th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Isaiah 40 is one of the great turning points of the Bible. For thirty-nine chapters the prophet warned of judgment. Now the people have lost everything and spent decades in exile in Babylon. And the first words God speaks are: "Comfort, O comfort my people." Speak tenderly. Tell her it's over. A voice calls to build a road through the desert, because God is coming home and bringing the exiles with him. People are like grass, the prophet says; they fade. "But the word of our God will stand forever." And God will come like a shepherd, carrying the lambs close to his heart. Revelation ends the Bible with a river and a tree: "and the leaves of the tree are for the healing of the nations."

What it means: After judgment comes comfort. God doesn't rub it in. The first word to a broken people is tenderness, and the promise that they'll be carried home.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Judaism: Isaiah 40 opens the Haftarah of Shabbat Nachamu, "the Sabbath of Comfort," read right after the fast for the Temple's destruction.
  • Buddha: Compassion, karuna, is the wish to ease another's suffering (paraphrased).
  • Hinduism: Today is Prabodhini Ekadashi, when Vishnu, the preserver, is said to wake and return.
  • Islam: Every chapter but one of the Qur'an opens "In the name of God, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful."

Carry it today: Whom can you speak tenderly to today?

The theme

  • Comfort, comfort: God's first word after exile.
  • A road in the desert: God is coming.
  • Grass and the word: What lasts.
  • The shepherd: Carrying the lambs.

Yesterday, Jacob called God the shepherd of his whole life. Today, Isaiah sees God coming as a shepherd for a whole people.

1. Comfort, O Comfort

"Comfort, O comfort my people, says your God."

  • Doubled: The repeated word is how Hebrew says "really, truly comfort."
  • "My people": After all the judgment, they're still his.

2. Speak Tenderly

"Speak tenderly to Jerusalem, and cry to her that she has served her term, that her penalty is paid."

  • Literally: "Speak to the heart of Jerusalem," the Hebrew phrase for wooing someone back.
  • It's over: The sentence has been served.

3. A Highway in the Wilderness

A voice cries out to prepare a straight road for God in the desert, raising every valley and leveling every mountain (Isaiah 40:3–4, paraphrased).

  • The way home: The road across the desert from Babylon to Jerusalem.
  • John the Baptist: All four Gospels apply this voice to John.

4. The Grass Withers

Human beings are like grass and wildflowers; they fade (Isaiah 40:6–7, paraphrased). "The grass withers; the flower fades, but the word of our God will stand forever."

  • Honest: The prophet doesn't pretend we last.
  • What does: God's promise outlives empires, Babylon included.

5. Like a Shepherd

God will feed his flock like a shepherd, gather the lambs in his arms, carry them close, and gently lead the nursing ewes (Isaiah 40:11, paraphrased). Revelation finishes the picture: a river of life, a tree whose fruit comes every month, "and the leaves of the tree are for the healing of the nations." Comfort isn't only for one people. It's for everyone.

For Christians today

Isaiah 40 is one of the best-loved chapters in the Christian Bible.

  • The oldest readings: All four Gospels quote "the voice crying in the wilderness" for John the Baptist, preparing the way for Jesus.
  • Handel's Messiah: It opens with "Comfort ye my people," and "He shall feed his flock" comes from verse 11.
  • Advent: Many churches read Isaiah 40 on the second Sunday of Advent, which starts in nine days.
  • Catholic: Today's Gospel has Jesus cleansing the Temple, preparing God's house.
  • Episcopal: Edmund, today's commemoration, was a young English king killed by Viking invaders in 870 rather than renounce his faith.

Carry it today: Tell someone carrying a heavy load that it's not all on them.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: the waking of the Preserver.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Prabodhini Ekadashi, when Vishnu, the preserver and protector, is said to wake from four months of cosmic sleep. Weddings and new beginnings resume, and many begin Tulsi Vivah, the marriage of the sacred basil plant to Vishnu.
  • The Jain lens: Jains end the four-month monsoon retreat, chaturmas, around this time, when monks and nuns set out walking again.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Ekadashi: "the eleventh day."
  • Prabodhini: "the awakening."
  • Tulsi: "the incomparable one," the holy basil.
  • Chaturmas: "four months."

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"The universe is transformation: life is opinion." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.3, Long translation.) Isaiah says the grass withers; Marcus says everything changes. Both found comfort in the same place: not in things lasting, but in how we hold them. For Marcus, it's a calm mind; for Isaiah, a word that stands forever.

The time. Isaiah 40–55 was written around 540 BC, near the end of the Babylonian exile, shortly before Cyrus of Persia let the Jews go home (538 BC). Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. Most scholars think chapters 40–55 come from an anonymous prophet of the exile, called "Second Isaiah," writing some 150 years after Isaiah of Jerusalem.

What it meant then. For exiles who thought God had abandoned them, this was the news that the long punishment was over and the road home was open.

The allegorical readings. Christians read the voice as John the Baptist and the shepherd as Christ; Jewish tradition reads the double "comfort" as comfort for a double loss, both Temples.

What's debated or unknown. Exactly how many authors stand behind the book of Isaiah, and who the "voice" is.
