November 15th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: In Judges, Deborah sits under her palm tree judging Israel. She sends for Barak and gives him God's command: gather ten thousand men at Mount Tabor, and God will deliver Sisera into his hand. In Matthew, a master going on a journey entrusts his money to three servants: five talents, two, and one, "to each according to his ability." (A talent was a huge sum, many years' wages.) Two put the money to work and double it. The third buries his in the ground. When the master returns, he tells the first two, "Well done... enter into the joy of your master." The third says, "I was afraid," and hands back the buried talent.

What it means: The servant who failed didn't steal or waste anything. He just played it safe, because he was afraid. Jesus says fear can bury the very gifts we've been given. Deborah and Barak could have stayed home, too. Faith means taking what God has given and putting it to work.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." (Enchiridion 5.) The servant's fear came from his picture of the master.
  • Judaism: This week's portion, Vayetzei, sends Jacob out with nothing but a promise, and he wakes to say, God was in this place, and I did not know it.
  • Hinduism: The Gita says it is better to do your own duty imperfectly than another's well (3:35, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Qur'an says God does not burden a soul beyond what it can bear (2:286, paraphrased), "to each according to his ability."

Carry it today: What have you buried out of fear?

All week we've watched Israel forget and relapse. Today Deborah's story begins, and Jesus asks what we're doing with what we've been given.

1. Deborah Under the Palm

"At that time Deborah, a prophet, wife of Lappidoth, was judging Israel. She used to sit under the palm of Deborah between Ramah and Bethel in the hill country of Ephraim."

  • A prophet and a judge: The people came to her for judgment.
  • Under a tree: No palace or throne; justice in the open air.

2. Barak Is Called

"The Lord, the God of Israel, commands you, 'Position yourself at Mount Tabor, taking ten thousand from the tribe of Naphtali and the tribe of Zebulun.'"

  • A reluctant hero: In the next verse, Barak says he'll go only if Deborah goes with him.
  • Two tribes: Small numbers against nine hundred iron chariots.

3. To Each According to Ability

"To one he gave five talents, to another two, to another one, to each according to his ability."

  • A talent: A huge sum, around twenty years of a laborer's wages. Even one talent was a fortune.
  • Our word "talent": Comes from this parable.

4. Well Done

"Well done, good and trustworthy slave; you have been trustworthy in a few things; I will put you in charge of many things; enter into the joy of your master."

  • The same words: The two-talent servant hears exactly the same praise as the five-talent one.
  • The reward: Not just more work, but joy.

5. I Was Afraid

"So I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground. Here you have what is yours." The third servant thought he was being careful. In his day, burying money was considered the safest way to protect it. But he had misjudged his master as harsh, and his fear shaped everything. He gave back exactly what he received: nothing lost, nothing gained, nothing lived. Paul says we are "children of light." The opposite of faith here isn't doubt; it's fear that buries the gift.

For Christians today

The parable of the talents has shaped how Christians think about gifts and responsibility.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read the talents as spiritual gifts, the gospel itself, entrusted to be shared, not hoarded.
  • Catholic: Today's first reading, Proverbs 31, praises the capable woman whose hands are always at work, paired with the faithful servants of the parable.
  • Protestant and evangelical: The parable is often read as a call to stewardship: time, money and abilities all belong to God.
  • Orthodox: Today's Gospel of the Good Samaritan shows a talent spent well: one man's compassion, risked on a dangerous road.
  • Live debates today: Some modern readers see the third servant as a hero who refused to join an unjust master's system; most churches still read the master as God.

Carry it today: Use one gift you've been hiding.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 5.) The third servant said, "I knew that you were a harsh man," and acted on that belief. His fear came from his picture of the master, not from the master himself. Epictetus would say: check your judgment before it buries your gift.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Chhath Puja, when devotees, mostly from Bihar and eastern India, fast and stand in rivers to offer prayers to the setting sun, Surya, giver of life.
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that each soul has infinite potential, hidden by karma, to be uncovered by effort.

Today is Shukla Shashthi, then Shukla Saptami from around midnight India time, in the month of Kartika, with the moon moving from Uttara Ashadha into Shravana (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (Sanskrit and Bhojpuri):

  • Chhath: "sixth," from Shashthi, the sixth day.
  • Surya: the sun.
  • Saptami: "the seventh day."
  • Shravana: "hearing."

The time. Judges is set in the 12th century BC. Matthew was written around AD 80–90; 1 Thessalonians around AD 50.

What scholars broadly agree on. A talent was a unit of weight for silver, worth about 6,000 denarii, or some twenty years' wages. Luke has a similar parable (the pounds, Luke 19:11–27).

What it meant then. For Matthew's church, waiting for Christ's return, the parable meant: don't just wait; be faithful and productive in the meantime.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the five talents as the five senses, or the gifts of the Spirit, and the buried talent as knowledge hidden instead of shared.

What's debated or unknown. Whether Jesus first told this parable about Israel's leaders, who guarded the Law but didn't share it, is debated.
