November 14th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: After the cycle of forgetting comes a song. Israel is oppressed by Jabin, a Canaanite king, and his general Sisera with nine hundred iron chariots. The prophet Deborah, who judges Israel under a palm tree, calls Barak to lead the tribes into battle. God sends a storm, the chariots bog down, and Israel wins. Now Deborah and Barak sing. The song blesses God "when the people offer themselves willingly." The peasantry prospered again "because you arose, Deborah, arose as a mother in Israel." In Matthew, Jesus warns about a house swept clean but left empty: the evil spirit returns, with seven worse.

What it means: Getting rid of what's wrong isn't enough. A swept, empty house is an invitation. Israel's relapses came when the house was clean but empty. Deborah's song shows what fills it: people who offer themselves willingly, and a God worth singing about.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "So a man when he has done a good act, does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act." (Meditations 5.6, Long.)
  • Judaism: Today's Shabbat portion, Toldot, tells of Rebekah, another strong mother in Israel, who shaped her family's future.
  • Hinduism: The Gita calls for action offered freely to God, without clinging to its fruits (2:47, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Qur'an praises those who spend themselves in God's way, night and day, in secret and in public (2:274, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Where will you offer yourself willingly today?

All week the story has been about forgetting and relapse. Today, for the first time, the people rise, and they sing.

1. Deborah and Barak Sang

"Then Deborah and Barak son of Abinoam sang on that day."

  • One of the oldest poems: Many scholars think the Song of Deborah is among the oldest texts in the Bible.
  • Like Miriam: Like Miriam's song at the Red Sea, a woman leads Israel's victory song.

2. Offered Willingly

"When locks are long in Israel, when the people offer themselves willingly— bless the Lord!"

  • Long locks: Perhaps warriors who let their hair grow as a vow of dedication.
  • Volunteers: Not drafted, but willing. The song later shames the tribes who stayed home.

3. A Mother in Israel

"The peasantry prospered in Israel; they grew fat on plunder, because you arose, Deborah, arose as a mother in Israel."

  • A prophet and a judge: Deborah is the only judge who is also called a prophet.
  • A mother: Not a warrior queen, but one who cares for her people.

4. Down to the Gates

"Then down to the gates marched the people of the Lord."

  • The gates: Where a town's justice and life happened.
  • The people of the Lord: No longer a scattered set of tribes.

5. Awake, Awake

"Awake, awake, Deborah!" Yesterday Revelation said, "Blessed is the one who stays awake." Today Deborah is called to wake and sing. And Jesus warns about the house "empty, swept, and put in order": cleaning out the bad isn't the whole work. A soul needs to be filled, with God, with willing service, with song. Otherwise the old spirits come back with friends.

For Christians today

Deborah and the empty house speak to how faith grows.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers saw Deborah as a figure of the church, and her song as a hymn of Christ's victory.
  • Catholic: Today's optional memorial of Mary fits Deborah: both are celebrated as mothers of God's people.
  • Episcopal: Samuel Seabury, remembered today, became a bishop after the American Revolution only by going to Scotland when England wouldn't consecrate him, a willing offering that founded the Episcopal Church.
  • Orthodox: Today's apostle, Philip, answered Nathanael's doubt with three words: "Come and see" (John 1:46).
  • Live debates today: Deborah's leadership is often cited in debates over women's leadership in churches.

Carry it today: Volunteer for something no one asked you to do.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"As a horse when he has run, a dog when he has tracked the game, a bee when it has made the honey, so a man when he has done a good act, does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act, as a vine goes on to produce again the grapes in season." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.6, Long translation.) Marcus pictures goodness as natural as a bee making honey, and the name Deborah means "bee." The willing offering doesn't look for applause; it just goes on to the next good act.

The time. The events are set in the 12th century BC. Matthew was written around AD 80–90.

What scholars broadly agree on. The Song of Deborah (Judges 5) is likely older than the prose story in Judges 4, and may be one of the oldest pieces of Hebrew poetry.

What it meant then. The song shows Israel as a loose alliance of tribes, some answering the call and some not, long before there was a king.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read Deborah, whose name means "bee," as the sweetness of prophecy, and Barak, "lightning," as the swift power of God.

What's debated or unknown. Many lines of the song are obscure, and translations differ widely, even on the opening "when locks are long."
